\begin{abstract}
Track reconstruction in the LHCb vertex detector can be written as a linear
system $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ over track \emph{segments}, whose solution
scores each segment by how strongly its neighbours reinforce it. Two earlier
papers solved this system with quantum linear algebra: first with the HHL
algorithm, and then far more cheaply with a one-bit variant, the one-bit
quantum filter (1BQF), which replaces the full inversion by a single cosine
filter of the Hamiltonian with one spectral zero. In this work I develop
that idea further. The 1BQF is the degree-one member of the family of
polynomial spectral transforms provided by the quantum singular value
transformation (QSVT), and once the problem is viewed this way the design
question changes: it is no longer a question of which algorithm inverts
$A$, but of which function of $\lambda$ separates true from false segments.
On clean events the answer is a four-line comb pinned to the eigenvalues of
a five-hit track. Read, as every solver in this work is, at the
efficiency-first working point (the lowest threshold that keeps $99\%$ of
the true segments), it holds the segment false rate below the classical
solver's up to $T\approx400$ tracks at the wide fixed $2$\,mrad acceptance;
at $T{=}1000$, where tangles of false segments have entered the clean-event
design's passes, the same degree-40 polynomial with its coefficients refit
to the events reads a $2.8\%$ false rate where the classical solver reads
$10.0\%$, and at the formula acceptance the same multiplicity is solved
outright ($99.98\%$ efficiency at a false rate below $0.1\%$). On noisy
events the answer must be measured: the false weight piles up on the
eigenvalue lines of specific two- and three-segment motifs, and a
polynomial fitted with roots at the measured pile-up beats the classical
baseline at every attainable efficiency below a sharply defined
ceiling. That ceiling has a structural cause: a broken track's
two-segment fragment and an accidental false pair are the same object
as far as $A$ is concerned --- two segments sharing one hit, hence the
same sub-block, hence the same eigenvalues and the same amplitudes ---
so on that Hamiltonian no function of $\lambda$ can separate them, and
their population is what caps segment efficiency near $0.98$. I also show that the two natural Hamiltonian modifications
inherited from the classical literature can only be judged together with
the response applied to them. The Denby--Peterson occupancy term destroys
the one-bit filter, whose single zero cannot follow the spectrum it
creates; but paired with a response refitted to that spectrum it becomes
the strongest configuration in the study: at the matched $99\%$-efficiency
point ($T{=}200$) it cuts the false rate thirtyfold at moderate noise
($0.31$ to $0.010$), and at heavy noise by a fifth to a half --- both
figures depend on the polynomial degree, the moderate one reading
seventeenfold at degree $40$ against thirtyfold at degree $80$ --- and,
at small coupling, it breaks the twin degeneracy that sets the ceiling,
which is therefore a property of the base Hamiltonian and not of the
problem. The bifurcation (fork) term is likewise a regression under the
clean-event comb applied without refitting, and likewise a win once the
response is refit to the spectrum it creates, but lower on the
efficiency axis: below the ceiling it wins, and at the strict $99\%$
point both systems flood alike. The result is a two-part
design rule: the operator shapes the spectrum, and the fitted response
separates it; neither knob can be evaluated without the other.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Charged-particle track reconstruction is, at heart, a combinatorial
matching problem: a detector hands us a cloud of hits, and we must decide
which hits belong together. At the High-Luminosity LHC this cloud becomes
dense enough that the matching cost dominates real-time reconstruction
budgets, and the tracking community has responded by testing essentially
every alternative computing platform: GPUs, FPGAs, neural networks, and, in
the line of work continued here, quantum computers.

The lineage of this work is short and worth stating precisely. Nicotra et
al.~\cite{nicotra2023} showed that track reconstruction in a simplified
model of the LHCb vertex detector (VELO) can be phrased as a linear system
$A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ over track segments and solved with the
Harrow--Hassidim--Lloyd algorithm~\cite{hhl2009}; a restricted-phase-estimation
variant of that construction was subsequently reported in
ref.~\cite{trackhhl_epj}. The follow-up
paper~\cite{trackhhl} observed that the full inversion is overkill. The
interesting information is which segments are active, not their precise
weights, so a single-ancilla circuit, the one-bit quantum filter (1BQF),
suffices. The 1BQF applies one cosine function of the Hamiltonian, placed
so that its zero lands exactly on the eigenvalue shared by every isolated
fake segment, and reads out the survivors. It is cheap, it suits near-term
hardware, and it was validated against device-calibrated noise models
of trapped-ion and superconducting processors; running it on the
devices themselves remains to be done.

This work asks the question that follows naturally from the 1BQF. A
cosine with one zero is a polynomial filter of degree
one\footnote{Explicitly, from the circuit of
section~\ref{sec:onebit}: the interferometer keeps the branch
$\tfrac12\big(I+e^{iAt}\big)=e^{iAt/2}\cos(At/2)$, and the leading
unitary drops out of the read-out of $|\langle j|\psi\rangle|$, so the
device applies $\cos(At/2)$ exactly. In the variable
$u=\cos(\lambda t/2)$ that response \emph{is} the polynomial
$p(u)=u$: degree one, and therefore one root, at $u=0$, i.e.\ at
$\lambda=\pi/t=s$. Each further application of the same controlled
evolution buys one more degree and with it one more root;
section~\ref{sec:qsvt} gives the general construction, in which $d$
calls of a fixed walk operator realise any bounded polynomial of
degree $d$.}. The quantum singular value transformation
(QSVT)~\cite{gslw2019,martyn2021} tells us that a quantum computer can
apply essentially any bounded polynomial of the Hamiltonian at a cost
linear in its degree. So, what could we do with more than one zero? Which
polynomial should we choose? And what happens to that choice when the
Hamiltonian itself changes, because the detector is noisy or because we
have added constraint terms to help it? In my opinion this last question
carries most of the physics, and most of this work is spent answering
it.

I answer these questions on the same toy model as the two predecessor
papers, extended with realistic noise. The answers can be summarised as a
single working method, which I will refer to as fitting the response to
the spectrum:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item On clean events the true-track spectrum is \emph{discrete}: detector
geometry pins every true track to the same five-hit chain, whose four
eigenvalues are known in closed form. A comb of narrow passes at those
four lines, and nothing else, separates true from false essentially
perfectly at low and moderate multiplicity; at the densities I can
simulate ($T=1000$ tracks, four million segments) and the wide fixed
$2$\,mrad acceptance, where the classical solver's false rate at
$99\%$ efficiency has climbed to $10.0\%$, the comb's coefficients must
be refit to the events, and then read $2.8\%$.
\item On noisy events the false population moves: it concentrates on the
eigenvalue lines of specific small \emph{motifs} (hit-sharing pairs and
triples), which sit off the true lines but inside any naive pass window.
The correct response cannot be written down a priori; it must be fitted
to the measured pile-up. Once it is, the quantum false rate drops below
the classical one at every efficiency below a ceiling that I can
identify, count, and explain segment by segment.
\item Hamiltonian engineering and response engineering are coupled, and
neither knob can be evaluated without the other. Both Denby--Peterson
penalties read as regressions under the clean-event comb applied
without refitting its coefficients, and both are rehabilitated once
those coefficients are refit to the spectrum they produce --- but not
equally, and not at the same place on the efficiency axis. On the
one-bit filter the two act in opposite directions: the fork term helps
it, while the occupancy term destroys it outright, its single zero
having no way to follow the co-hit coupling the term creates. The
occupancy term is the sharpest case in the study: with the polynomial's
coefficients refit it cuts the false rate at the matched
$99\%$-efficiency point thirtyfold at moderate noise and by a fifth to
a half at heavy noise ($T{=}200$), both of them degree-dependent --- at
degree $40$ the moderate figure is seventeenfold, at degree $80$
thirtyfold --- and at small coupling it breaks the twin degeneracy that
caps segment efficiency near $0.98$ on the base Hamiltonian. The fork
term wins too, but only below that cap. Changing the operator moves
the activations, and moving the activations changes the polynomial we
have to fit.
\end{enumerate}

This work is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:toy} builds the toy
model and the segment Hamiltonian from zero: the event generator and the
noise triple it takes, the exact activation levels that make everything
else quantitative, and the conventions every later number obeys --- the
efficiency-first working point, and the definitions of segment
efficiency and segment false rate. Section~\ref{sec:onebit} reads the
1BQF as a spectral filter, deriving what its single zero removes, what
it leaves behind, and how it reshapes the true band.
Section~\ref{sec:qsvt} develops the QSVT machinery (block encoding,
qubitization, and the linear-combination-of-Chebyshev construction) at
the level of detail needed to count qubits and gates.

Section~\ref{sec:comb} designs and evaluates the comb on clean events,
including its resource scaling and the twin degeneracy that limits every
response in the family. Section~\ref{sec:noise} moves to the heavy
operating point and measures the structure the noise creates: no giant
tangle, but a population of small false motifs.
Section~\ref{sec:occupancy} dissects the occupancy term's collision with
the notch. Section~\ref{sec:fork} introduces the fork term and the
design rule that every operator change demands a response refit.
Section~\ref{sec:fit} fits the polynomial to the measured false pile-up
and presents the main comparison, including the refitted occupancy
result. Section~\ref{sec:atscale} then crosses the two axes
systematically --- every Hamiltonian against every polynomial degree,
with the coefficients refit at each --- and reports the loss budget, the
density scan that carries every solver to $T=1000$, and the two-axis
rule the study reduces to. Section~\ref{sec:limits} collects the
limitations: that the occupancy win does not survive density, the
realizability of a refitted response, the single-response question,
Trotterization, the hardware-resource comparison of HHL, the 1BQF and
the QSVT --- measured for the latter two, a structure-level estimate for
HHL --- with the error-correction requirements each implies, a
hardware-native block encoding priced by detector hits rather than
matrix entries, the success probability and readout cost, what the
classical simulability of the response family does and does not concede,
and what a real detector geometry changes.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

A remark on style before we begin. This work is deliberately verbose.
The predecessor papers compressed the segment-Hamiltonian formalism to a
few paragraphs; here I want every level, every eigenvalue, and every
convention on the table, because the physics of why filters succeed and
fail lives in exactly those details.

\section{The toy model and the segment Hamiltonian}
\label{sec:toy}

\subsection{Events}
\label{sec:toy:events}

The toy detector is the standard simplified VELO of
refs.~\cite{nicotra2023,trackhhl}: parallel planes perpendicular to the
beam axis ($z$), each recording a two-dimensional point where a charged
particle crosses it. Every parameter of the generator is fixed for the
whole of this work, and I list them here so that any event in it can be
reproduced: five planes at $z=33,\,66,\,99,\,132,\,165$\,mm, so the
inter-plane spacing is $\Delta z=33$\,mm, each plane square of half-width
$40$\,mm in $x$ and $y$; $T$ tracks per event from a common primary
vertex, itself drawn on the beam axis with $\sigma_z=1$\,mm and no
transverse spread; and generator angles bounded by
$\varphi_{\max}=\theta_{\max}=0.2$\,rad, so the tracks fill a cone of
half-opening $0.2$. Each track is propagated as a straight line and
leaves one hit per plane.

Detector effects enter as controlled perturbations, and I will always
state the triple in force: multiple-scattering kinks of width
$\sigma_{\rm scatt}$ applied at each plane crossing, Gaussian
hit-position smearing of width $\sigma_{\rm res}$, and a hit-drop
(inefficiency) probability applied hit by hit. Three configurations are
used in this work and no others:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
 & $\sigma_{\rm scatt}$ & $\sigma_{\rm res}$ & drop & $\varepsilon$\\
\midrule
clean    & $10^{-4}$ & $0$            & $0$    & $0.42$\,mrad\\
moderate & $10^{-4}$ & $10\,\mu$m     & $1\%$  & $3.18$\,mrad\\
heavy    & $10^{-4}$ & $20\,\mu$m     & $1\%$  & $6.31$\,mrad\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
where $\varepsilon$ is the angular acceptance that
eq.~\eqref{eq:epsilon} below derives from each triple. Clean events
carry section~\ref{sec:comb}; the heavy configuration is the operating
point from section~\ref{sec:noise} onward; the moderate one is the
intermediate rung used wherever sections~\ref{sec:fit}
and~\ref{sec:atscale} scan across noise.

A \emph{segment} is an ordered pair of hits on adjacent planes. With five
planes and $T$ hits per plane, each of the four inter-plane gaps offers
$T^2$ candidate segments, so
\begin{equation}
n_{\rm seg}=4T^{2},\qquad n_{\rm true}=4T,\qquad
n_{\rm false}=4T(T-1),
\label{eq:combinatorics}
\end{equation}
where $n_{\rm true}$ counts the segments that connect two hits of the
same generated track. The problem is a needle in a haystack, and the
ratio $n_{\rm true}/n_{\rm seg}=1/T$ says how deep the hay is: at
$T=1000$ only one segment in a thousand is real, and the false population
grows quadratically while the true one grows linearly. Any segment
classifier, classical or quantum, fights this composition before it
fights anything else.

\subsection{The Hamiltonian}
\label{sec:toy:ham}

Following Denby and Peterson's neural formulation of track
finding~\cite{denby1988,peterson1989} as adapted by
refs.~\cite{nicotra2023,trackhhl}, we encode an event in a quadratic
energy over segment activations $x_i\in\mathbb{R}$,
\begin{equation}
E(\mathbf{x})=\tfrac12\,\mathbf{x}^{\!\top}\!A\,\mathbf{x}
-\delta\,\mathbf{1}^{\!\top}\mathbf{x},
\qquad
A=(\gamma+\delta)\,I-C,
\label{eq:energy}
\end{equation}
where $C$ is the 0/1 \emph{compatibility adjacency}: $C_{ij}=1$ exactly
when segments $i$ and $j$ share a hit as head-to-tail continuations (the
end hit of one is the start hit of the other) and their mutual angle is
below an acceptance $\varepsilon$. The constants are a self-penalty
$\gamma$ (discouraging indiscriminate activation) and a bias $\delta$
(rewarding activation); throughout the main text $\gamma=3$, $\delta=1$,
so the constant diagonal is $s\equiv\gamma+\delta=4$. Minimising
eq.~\eqref{eq:energy} gives the linear system
\begin{equation}
A\,\mathbf{x}=\delta\,\mathbf{1},
\label{eq:linsys}
\end{equation}
which I solve classically with MINRES. A useful picture is a voting
market: each segment starts with the same bias-funded credit $\delta$,
and the off-diagonal couplings let aligned neighbours reinforce one
another. A segment embedded in a long, straight chain of compatible
neighbours accumulates support; a segment with no compatible continuation
is left with only its own bias against its own penalty.

Whether that system is well posed at all is something the two constants
buy, and it is worth being exact about how. Since $A$'s diagonal is the
constant $s$ and $C$ is a zero-diagonal $0/1$ adjacency, the two spectra
are related not by a bound but by an identity,
$\lambda(A)=s-\lambda(C)$, so
\begin{equation}
\lambda_{\min}(A)=(\gamma+\delta)-\lambda_{\max}(C),
\label{eq:lammin}
\end{equation}
and $A$ is positive definite exactly when $\lambda_{\max}(C)<\gamma+\delta$.
Gershgorin applied to $C$ turns that into something checkable without
solving anything: $\lambda_{\max}(C)\le d_{\max}$, the largest number of
compatible neighbours any segment has, so $\gamma+\delta>d_{\max}$ is
sufficient. Positive definiteness is therefore not a property one hopes
for but one the regularisation controls: on any event, raising $\gamma$
past $\lambda_{\max}(C)-\delta$ restores it.

The sufficient condition is loose, and the honest statement is that
sparsity, not the bound, is what keeps the benchmark solvable. At
$\varepsilon=2$\,mrad and $\gamma=3$ some segment already reaches degree
$4=s$ by $T\approx400$, so the Gershgorin floor $s-d_{\max}$ collapses to
zero there, while the measured $\lambda_{\min}(A)$ is still $0.8$--$2.4$
out to $T=1000$ (figure~\ref{fig:spectrum}). Where it does bite is the wide-acceptance corner. At the
heavy configuration, evaluated exactly on each connected component,
$\lambda_{\min}(A)$ falls from $1.91$ at $T=100$ through $0.71$ at
$T=400$ to $0.10\pm0.26$ at $T=700$ and $0.009$ at $T=1000$: marginal,
with the occasional negative mode already appearing at $T=700$. Push the
resolution to $\sigma_{\rm res}=50\,\mu$m, where
eq.~\eqref{eq:epsilon} returns $\varepsilon=15.8$\,mrad, and
$\lambda_{\max}(C)$ crosses $\gamma+\delta$ near $T\approx290$: beyond
that $A$ is genuinely indefinite, and the classical solution begins to
diverge on the offending cluster rather than merely being ill-conditioned.
This is why the linear system is solved with MINRES rather than the
conjugate gradient that a positive-definite matrix would invite: the same
code path has to serve those corners and the $\gamma=1$ operating point,
where $2I-C$ is indefinite outright and conjugate gradient has no
convergence guarantee at all.

The picture behind that argument is worth drawing, because it makes visible
exactly where the loose step is (figure~\ref{fig:gershgorin}). Every row of $A$
has the same diagonal $s$ and $d_i$ off-diagonal entries equal to $-1$, so every
Gershgorin disc is a circle centred at $s$ of radius $d_i$: a segment with no
compatible neighbour contributes a disc of radius zero, a point pinned at $s$,
and each further neighbour grows the disc by one. The condition fails precisely
when some disc first reaches the origin, which is $d_i\ge s$. The measured
degree distribution then shows why the failure is so rarely real: at $T=200$,
$99.5\%$ of segments on clean events and still $98.6\%$ under the heavy
configuration have no compatible neighbour at all, and at the heavy
configuration exactly $11$ segments out of $157{,}609$ reach $d_i\ge4=s$. The
bound is a statement about the worst segment in an event; solvability is a
statement about the typical one, and at these occupancies the two are very far
apart.

The knob, by contrast, is exact rather than sufficient. Since
$\lambda(A)=s-\mu(C)$ and $\mu_{\max}(C)$ does not depend on $\gamma$ at all,
\begin{equation}
\lambda_{\min}(A)=\gamma+\delta-\mu_{\max}(C)
\label{eq:gammaknob}
\end{equation}
is a straight line in $\gamma$ of unit slope, crossing zero at
$\gamma=\mu_{\max}(C)-\delta$. One measures $\mu_{\max}(C)$ once on an event and
reads off the self-penalty that guarantees positive definiteness; there is
nothing to hope for. Measured this way the working value $\gamma=3$ has room to
spare everywhere in this work: the crossing sits at $\gamma=2.24$ for the heavy
configuration at $T=400$ and at $\gamma=2.58$ at $T=700$, the hardest point
reached, leaving $\lambda_{\min}=0.76$ and $0.42$ respectively.

The same operator is worth writing in the form the predecessor papers
use, because the two look quite different on the page.
Refs.~\cite{nicotra2023,trackhhl_epj,trackhhl} build the energy term by
term in the Denby--Peterson manner, over activations $S_i$ that begin
binary and are then relaxed to the reals:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{H}(S)=\mathcal{H}_{\rm ang}(S,\varepsilon)
+\gamma\,\mathcal{H}_{\rm spec}(S)+\delta\,\mathcal{H}_{\rm gap}(S),
\label{eq:dpform}
\end{equation}
an angular term that rewards compatible continuations,
\[
\mathcal{H}_{\rm ang}=-\tfrac12\sum_{i,j}f(\theta_{ij},\varepsilon)\,S_iS_j,
\qquad
f(\theta,\varepsilon)=
\begin{cases}1,&\cos\theta\ge1-\varepsilon,\\[2pt] 0,&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}
\]
plus a spectral term $\mathcal{H}_{\rm spec}=\tfrac12\sum_iS_i^{2}$ that
penalises activation and a gap term
$\mathcal{H}_{\rm gap}=\tfrac12\sum_i(1-S_i)^{2}$ that rewards it. In
refs.~\cite{trackhhl_epj,trackhhl} the two multipliers are called $\alpha$
and $\beta$; they are the $\gamma$ and $\delta$ used here, as the
reference implementation of ref.~\cite{trackhhl} makes explicit.
Expanding the squares,
\[
\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{x})
=\tfrac12\,\mathbf{x}^{\!\top}\!\big[(\gamma+\delta)I-C\big]\mathbf{x}
-\delta\,\mathbf{1}^{\!\top}\mathbf{x}+\tfrac{\delta}{2}\,n_{\rm seg}
=E(\mathbf{x})+\tfrac{\delta}{2}\,n_{\rm seg},
\]
so eq.~\eqref{eq:energy} is that energy up to a constant independent of
$\mathbf{x}$: the same Hessian, the same gradient, the same minimiser,
and $\nabla\mathcal{H}=0$ is eq.~\eqref{eq:linsys}. What differs between
the papers is not the operator but three conventions, and they matter
whenever numbers are carried across. First, ref.~\cite{nicotra2023}
writes the Ising template
$\mathcal{H}=-\tfrac12\mathbf{x}^{\!\top}\!A\mathbf{x}
+\mathbf{b}^{\!\top}\mathbf{x}$, which for a positive-definite $A$ is
$-E(\mathbf{x})$ and hence concave; ref.~\cite{trackhhl} already uses the
convex sign adopted here. Second, their acceptance is a cosine tolerance,
$\cos\theta\ge1-\varepsilon$, where mine is an angle in radians; the two
are related by $\varepsilon_{\cos}=1-\cos\varepsilon\simeq\varepsilon^2/2$,
so the $10^{-5}$ of ref.~\cite{nicotra2023} is $4.5$\,mrad, and the
$6.3$\,mrad of the heavy configuration here is $2.0\times10^{-5}$ in
their variable. Third, and most consequential for every closed form
below, they take $\gamma=2$ where this work takes $\gamma=3$, so their
$s=3$: the isolated attractor sits at $\tfrac13$ rather than $\tfrac14$,
a five-hit track at $(\tfrac35,\tfrac45)$ rather than
$(\tfrac{4}{11},\tfrac{5}{11})$, and a long chain spans $[1,5]$ rather
than $[2,6]$ --- which is why refs.~\cite{trackhhl_epj,trackhhl} quote a
spectrum bounded by $\lambda_{\min}\approx1$, $\lambda_{\max}\approx5$
and a filter notch at $\lambda=\gamma+\delta=3$. Every level in this work
is stated at $s=4$.

The acceptance $\varepsilon$ deserves its own derivation, because it is
the single most consequential experimental knob and because the formula
that sets it is not a convention: it is a quantile of the kink
distribution the detector actually produces.

Take three consecutive hits of one true track, on planes $k{-}1$, $k$,
$k{+}1$, and the kink between the two segments they form
(figure~\ref{fig:epsilon}a). Two things disturb it. At the middle plane
the particle is deflected by multiple scattering, a Gaussian angle of
width $\sigma_{\rm scatt}$ \emph{in each transverse projection}, which
adds straight onto the kink. And each of the three hits is recorded with
an independent Gaussian position error $\sigma_{\rm res}$; in one
projection the measured kink is the second difference
\[
\theta_x=\frac{x_3-x_2}{\Delta z}-\frac{x_2-x_1}{\Delta z}
        =\frac{x_1-2x_2+x_3}{\Delta z},
\]
whose $(1,-2,1)$ weights give it variance
$(1^2+2^2+1^2)\,\sigma_{\rm res}^{2}/\Delta z^{2}
=6\,\sigma_{\rm res}^{2}/\Delta z^{2}$: the factor $6$ is the stencil,
not a fudge. The two sources are independent, so each projection of the
kink is Gaussian of width
\[
\sigma_p^{2}=\sigma_{\rm scatt}^{2}
+6\,\arctan^{2}\!\big(\sigma_{\rm res}/\Delta z\big).
\]
A track lives in three dimensions, so what the angular cut acts on is
the magnitude $\theta=\sqrt{\theta_x^{2}+\theta_y^{2}}$ of two
independent Gaussian projections. That magnitude is
\emph{Rayleigh}-distributed with scale $\sigma_p$, not Gaussian, and it
is the Rayleigh tail, $P(\theta>\varepsilon)
=\exp(-\varepsilon^{2}/2\sigma_p^{2})$, that decides how many true
continuations a cut at $\varepsilon$ discards.
Figure~\ref{fig:epsilon}b measures the kink distribution on generated
events and finds the closed form holds: at the heavy configuration the
measured mean kink is $1.84$\,mrad against the Rayleigh prediction
$\sigma_p\sqrt{\pi/2}=1.86$\,mrad.

Admitting kinks out to $\kappa$ standard deviations of a projection
means $\varepsilon=\kappa\sqrt2\,\sigma_p$, which written out is
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon=\sqrt{\,2\,(\kappa\sigma_{\rm scatt})^{2}
+12\,\arctan^{2}\!\big(\kappa\sigma_{\rm res}/\Delta z\big)
+2\,\theta_{\min}^{2}\,},
\label{eq:epsilon}
\end{equation}
with $\theta_{\min}=15\,\mu$rad a floor that keeps the acceptance finite
in the noise-free limit. The coefficients are the per-projection ones,
$1$ for scattering and $6$ for resolution, doubled by the two
projections. This work uses $\kappa=3$ throughout, and the reason to
write the cut this way is that it fixes the price: the fraction of true
continuations lost is $\exp(-\kappa^{2})=e^{-9}=1.2\times10^{-4}$
\emph{whatever} the noise, because $\varepsilon$ moves with $\sigma_p$.
Read backwards, $\varepsilon=\sigma_p\sqrt{2\ln(1/p)}$ sets the
acceptance from whatever miss rate $p$ one is willing to pay.

The three configurations give $\varepsilon=0.42$, $3.18$ and
$6.31$\,mrad; the heavy value is fifteen times the clean one, and
section~\ref{sec:noise} shows what that width admits. One
configuration in this work does \emph{not} take its acceptance from
eq.~\eqref{eq:epsilon}: the benchmark campaign of
section~\ref{sec:comb} holds $\varepsilon=2$\,mrad fixed on clean
events, which is $4.7$ times the formula value there, $20\sigma_p$
rather than $3\sqrt2\,\sigma_p$. Measured on a stored clean event at
$T=200$, the two cuts keep the same $600$ true continuations --- the
formula loses none of them --- while the wide one admits $71$ false
compatibility edges against $5$, and at $T=400$, $661$ against $37$. The
benchmark is therefore a deliberate stress test of the false
population, not a physics-derived operating point, and it is labelled as
fixed wherever it is used.

\subsection{From tracks to graphs}
\label{sec:toy:graphs}

Every structural argument in this work is an argument about a graph, so it is
worth fixing once, and explicitly, which graph is meant and how a detector
object becomes one.

An event is first a layered graph of \emph{hits}: the five planes are the
layers, each recorded hit is a vertex, and each candidate segment is an edge
joining a hit on one plane to a hit on the next. The object the Hamiltonian
acts on is the graph one level up from that. Its vertices are the
\emph{segments} --- the edges downstairs --- and two of them are joined when
they share a hit head to tail and their mutual angle lies inside the acceptance
$\varepsilon$. It is, in other words, the line graph of the hit graph, thinned
by the angular window, and the compatibility adjacency $C$ of
eq.~\eqref{eq:energy} is its adjacency matrix. A segment is an edge below and a
vertex above; that change of level is the one thing to keep straight, because
everything from here on is stated upstairs. A \emph{cluster} means a connected
component of the upstairs graph, and since $A=sI-C$ is block diagonal over
those components, every eigenvalue quoted anywhere in this work is an
eigenvalue of one cluster. Figure~\ref{fig:correspondence} walks four clusters
through the entire chain: what each looks like in the detector, the graph it
becomes, the block of $A$ it contributes, and the eigenvalues that block
carries.

A perfect track is the simplest case. Five hits give four segments, each
continuing the next and nothing else, so a true track is a path on four
vertices --- the graph $P_4$. Tracks do not stay perfect. A dropped hit deletes
the two segments that touch it, and the path breaks; with five hits there are
only four outcomes, and between them they generate the whole vocabulary of
shorter motifs (figure~\ref{fig:fragmentation}):

\end{center}
(losing the fourth hit mirrors losing the second, and losing the last mirrors
losing the first). The activations are those of section~\ref{sec:toy:levels}
below, and the last row is already the whole difficulty of this work in one
line: a true track that loses its middle hit leaves two segments sitting at
exactly the activation of a false isolated segment.

Three graph families cover essentially every cluster that occurs, and each is
given its name and its graph here, once, so that later sections can carry both:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item an \textbf{isolated segment} is the graph $P_1$ (one vertex, no edge): a
segment with no compatible continuation at all;
\item a \textbf{chain} is a path graph $P_m$ ($m$ vertices in a line): $m$
segments each continuing the next. Short chains have the names the figures use
--- a \emph{pair} is $P_2$, two segments meeting at a shared hit, and a
\emph{triple} is $P_3$. A triple arises two physically distinct ways that the
graph cannot tell apart, three segments in a row or one segment continued by
two others, and this is the first appearance of the degeneracy that
section~\ref{sec:comb:limits} turns into a theorem;
\item a \textbf{hub}, or star, is the complete bipartite graph $K_{1,m}$ (a
centre joined to $m$ leaves, the leaves joined to nothing): one segment
continued by $m$ others which do not continue one another.
\end{enumerate}
A true track is the chain $P_4$; every other member of these families, at the
sizes that occur, is either a fragment of a broken track or an accident of the
false population.

That these families suffice is a measurement, not an assumption, and it is
worth making because nothing forces a cluster to be any of them --- or even to
be a tree. Two segments that both end on one hit, together with two that both
start from it, form a four-cycle if all four pairs are compatible, and larger
tangles are possible in principle. Counting every connected component of the
compatibility graph over three events at each configuration gives the census of
table~\ref{tab:census}. Almost every cluster is a single isolated segment, and among the rest the three families account for 99.4\% of components at the heavy configuration at $T=1000$: chains dominate, hubs appear once the acceptance is wide enough for one segment to have several continuations, and the residue is a scatter of larger trees. Cycles do occur --- they are not forbidden, only rare --- and reach 0.61\% of multi-segment clusters at their worst (heavy, $T=1000$). Read on the true population instead of on components, the same census gives the number that section~\ref{sec:fit} has to live with: at the heavy configuration and $T=1000$, only 46.1\% of true segments still sit in a complete four-segment chain, 1.7\% have been broken down into shorter fragments, and 52.0\% have been absorbed into clusters of five segments or more by false neighbours --- tangles whose eigenvalues are not the four true lines at all.

\subsection{Exact activation levels: the atlas}
\label{sec:toy:levels}

Because $A$ is a diagonal matrix minus the compatibility adjacency,
it is block diagonal over the connected components of that graph: it couples
no segment to anything outside its own cluster, so the whole system splits
into one independent small problem per cluster, and those problems can be
solved by hand. (That the diagonal is not merely constant \emph{per cluster}
but the same constant $s$ everywhere buys something further, used in
section~\ref{sec:toy:spectrum}: the spectra of $A$ and of the graph are
related by the rigid shift $\lambda(A)=s-\mu(C)$ rather than cluster by
cluster.) This is worth doing once in full, because these little
closed forms are the vocabulary of every result in this work.

An \emph{isolated} segment (no compatible neighbour) has the scalar
equation $(\gamma+\delta)x=\delta$, so
\begin{equation}
x_{\rm iso}=\frac{\delta}{\gamma+\delta}=\frac14=0.250 .
\label{eq:iso}
\end{equation}
This is the false \emph{attractor}: at any multiplicity, the
overwhelming majority of the $4T(T-1)$ false segments are isolated and
sit at exactly $0.250$. A true track, by contrast, is a path $P_4$ of
four segments (five hits), whose matrix is $sI-P_4$ with $P_4$ the path
adjacency. Its solution can be written down exactly:
\begin{equation}
x_{\rm outer}=\frac{4}{11}\approx0.364,\qquad
x_{\rm inner}=\frac{5}{11}\approx0.455 ,
\label{eq:p4levels}
\end{equation}
where the outer levels belong to the two end segments (one continuation
neighbour each) and the inner levels to the two middle segments (two
neighbours each).

These numbers, and the whole family behind them, come from one small
calculation that is worth doing explicitly. A chain of $m$ segments
has the matrix $sI-P_m$ with $P_m$ the path adjacency, so the $j$-th
row of $(sI-P_m)\,\mathbf{x}=\delta\mathbf{1}$ reads
\[
-x_{j-1}+s\,x_j-x_{j+1}=\delta ,\qquad j=1,\dots,m ,
\]
a three-term linear recurrence, provided we adopt the convention
$x_0=x_{m+1}=0$: an end segment simply has no neighbour on one side,
which is the same as coupling it to a phantom segment clamped at
zero. A recurrence of this kind is solved by any particular solution
plus the general homogeneous one. The natural particular solution is
the constant
\[
\bar x=\frac{\delta}{s-2}=\frac12 \qquad (s=4,\ \delta=1),
\]
the activation of a segment deep inside an infinitely long chain,
where both neighbours carry the same value and the row equation
collapses to $(s-2)\,\bar x=\delta$. The homogeneous solutions of
$-x_{j-1}+s\,x_j-x_{j+1}=0$ are the geometric sequences $r^{\,j}$
with
\[
r+\frac{1}{r}=s
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
r=\frac{s-\sqrt{s^{2}-4}}{2}=2-\sqrt3\approx0.268 ,
\]
a decaying root and its growing mirror $1/r$. Writing the general
solution as $x_j=\bar x+A\,r^{\,j}+B\,r^{\,m+1-j}$ (one geometric
correction anchored at each end) and imposing the two clamped-end
conditions $x_0=x_{m+1}=0$ forces, by the chain's reflection
symmetry, $A=B=-1/\big(2\,(1+r^{m+1})\big)$, so the entire
activation profile has the closed form
\[
x_j=\frac12-\frac{r^{\,j}+r^{\,m+1-j}}{2\,(1+r^{m+1})} .
\]
The picture behind the algebra: each end of the chain is a cold
boundary that drags the activation below the bulk value $\tfrac12$
by a deficit decaying geometrically into the interior, with
penetration depth $1/\ln(1/r)\approx0.76$ segments. Setting $j=1$
(or, identically, $j=m$) gives the outer activation
\begin{equation}
x_{\rm outer}(m)=\frac12-\frac{r+r^{m}}{2\,(1+r^{m+1})},
\qquad r=2-\sqrt3,
\label{eq:ladder}
\end{equation}
and $j=2$ at $m=4$ returns the inner $5/11$ of
eq.~\eqref{eq:p4levels}. Equation~\eqref{eq:ladder} climbs the ladder
$\{0.250,\ \tfrac13,\ \tfrac{5}{14},\
\tfrac{4}{11},\dots\}\nearrow(1-r)/2=(\sqrt3-1)/2\approx0.366$ for
$m=1,2,3,4,\dots$: a short chain sits low because the two boundary
deficits overlap, and the ladder saturates once the ends are further
apart than the penetration depth. It is worth
working two rungs by hand. A two-hit fragment ($m{=}1$) sits at exactly
the isolated-false attractor $0.250$, and a three-hit fragment ($m{=}2$)
sits at $1/3$, only $0.083$ above it. Fragments are barely
distinguishable from noise by amplitude alone; this innocent-looking
fact returns in section~\ref{sec:fit} as the ceiling on segment
efficiency.

The decision rule is a threshold: segment $i$ is \emph{active} iff
$x_i>\tau$. Every threshold choice trades efficiency against false
rate, and this work reads every solver, the classical inversion
included, at one working point, the \emph{efficiency-first} one: the
threshold is the lowest value that admits the true solutions, placed
just below the $1\%$ quantile of that solver's own true-segment
amplitudes so that $99\%$ of true segments survive by construction,
and the false rate at that threshold is the price quoted. An earlier
convention, inherited from ref.~\cite{trackhhl}, fixed an absolute
threshold $\tau=\delta/(\gamma+\delta)+0.10$ ($0.35$ at $\gamma=3$)
as a margin above the false attractor. It is not used here: a filter
reshapes the amplitude atlas, and that fixed cut lands inside the
one-bit filter's reshaped true band (section~\ref{sec:onebit}),
discarding true segments and understating the false rate at the same
time. Section~\ref{sec:onebit} shows the full trade-off explicitly in
figure~\ref{fig:threshold}.

\subsection{Which modes the bias can excite}
\label{sec:toy:modes}

The atlas gives each cluster's activation and section~\ref{sec:toy:spectrum}
gives each cluster's eigenvalues, but the two are joined by something the
eigenvalues alone do not say: how much of each mode the bias actually puts
there. Every filter in this work acts on eigenvalues, so this is exactly where
a filter's leverage comes from, and it is worth being exact about it.

Segments are the \emph{coordinates} of the problem, not its modes. The basis
vector $\mathbf{e}_i$ --- ``segment $i$ active and nothing else'' --- is an
eigenvector of $A$ only when segment $i$ is isolated, in which case
$A\mathbf{e}_i=s\,\mathbf{e}_i$ and the identification of a segment with a mode
is exact. That case covers the overwhelming majority of segments, which is why
the identification is tempting, and it fails for every cluster that matters. A
cluster of $m$ mutually compatible segments spans an $m$-dimensional invariant
subspace on which $A$ acts as $sI_m-C_{\rm cluster}$, and the eigenvectors of
that block are spread over the whole cluster. A true track therefore does not
have \emph{an} eigenvalue: it owns a four-dimensional block whose spectrum is
the four lines of eq.~\eqref{eq:p4lines}, and each of the four modes lives on
all four segments at once, as the standing waves
$v_k(j)\propto\sin\!\big(jk\pi/(m{+}1)\big)$ of a particle in a box.

The track's activation is a superposition of those modes,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}\big|_{\rm cluster}
=\sum_{k}\frac{\langle v_k,\mathbf{b}\rangle}{\lambda_k}\,v_k ,
\label{eq:modesum}
\end{equation}
and the overlaps $\langle v_k,\mathbf{b}\rangle$ decide which modes appear at
all. Here the uniformity of the bias does something strong. Because
$\mathbf{b}=\delta\mathbf{1}$ is invariant under every automorphism of the
cluster's graph, it lies entirely in the trivial representation of that
automorphism group: only modes symmetric under every relabelling that preserves
the graph can be excited, and every other mode has
$\langle v_k,\mathbf{b}\rangle=0$ \emph{exactly}. Call a mode \emph{bright}
when that overlap is non-zero and \emph{dark} when it vanishes. A dark mode
carries an eigenvalue but no amplitude --- present in the spectrum, absent from
the solution --- and no choice of filter can change that, since $f(A)\mathbf{b}$
has the same overlaps as $A^{-1}\mathbf{b}$; the filter reweights modes, it
cannot create one.

For a chain the consequence is severe. A path $P_m$ has one non-trivial
automorphism, the reflection $j\mapsto m{+}1-j$, so all its
reflection-antisymmetric modes are dark and only $\lceil m/2\rceil$ of its $m$
lines are bright. For the true track this halves the picture: of the four lines
$\{2.382,\,3.382,\,4.618,\,5.618\}$ only the first and third are bright, and the
exact solution $(\tfrac{4}{11},\tfrac{5}{11},\tfrac{5}{11},\tfrac{4}{11})$ is
assembled from those two alone --- $98.5\%$ of its weight on $\lambda=2.382$ and
the remaining $1.5\%$ on $\lambda=4.618$ (figure~\ref{fig:trackmodes}). A star
$K_{1,m}$ is more extreme still: its automorphism group permutes all $m$
leaves, so the trivial component is two-dimensional whatever $m$ is, and a hub
of any size has exactly two bright lines, $s\pm\sqrt m$, with all $m{-}1$ copies
of $\lambda=s$ dark.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\captionsetup{width=0.95\textwidth}
\includegraphics[width=0.98\textwidth]{track_modes.pdf}
\caption{What a true track's four eigenvalues actually carry. (a) The track and
its graph, the chain $P_4$. (b--e) The four modes of that chain, drawn as their
weight on each segment: every mode is spread over all four segments, so no mode

\label{fig:trackmodes}

Two things follow that the rest of this work leans on. The first repairs what
would otherwise be a false statement about the spectrum. The triple $P_3$ has a
line at exactly $\lambda=s$, and a hub $K_{1,m}$ has $m{-}1$ of them, so these
false motifs do \emph{not} avoid the isolated line --- they sit precisely on it,
and precisely on the notch that section~\ref{sec:onebit}'s filter puts there.
What separates them from the isolated population is not position but darkness:
those coincident modes are unexcited, so nothing is lost when the notch removes
the line they occupy. The clean statement is that every false family's
\emph{bright} lines interleave the true lines without touching them, and its
dark lines may sit wherever they like.

The second is a design consequence. The comb of section~\ref{sec:comb} places a
pass at each of the four true lines, but two of those lines are dark: on clean
events, where every true track is an exact $P_4$, no true amplitude exists at
$\lambda=3.382$ or $\lambda=5.618$ for a tooth to pass, so those two teeth can
only admit whatever false weight happens to land there. Removing them costs
nothing and buys separation. Measured on clean events across $T=100$--$400$ at
the production degree $40$, the false rate is zero for either design --- it
cannot rank them --- but the separation margin, the ratio of the smallest true
amplitude to the largest false one, rises from $47$ with four teeth to $108$
with the two bright teeth alone; and because two teeth sit twice as far apart as
four, they can be widened from $h_w=0.18$ to $0.24$, which lifts the margin to
$750$. A control comb placed on the two \emph{dark} lines instead never
separates at all, at any degree tested (margin below $1$ throughout). The
advantage is a clean-event result and does not survive noise: once a track
carries an extra false segment it is no longer a symmetric chain, its dark lines
acquire overlap, and at the moderate and heavy configurations the two designs
are indistinguishable. Section~\ref{sec:fit} is where that is dealt with, by
refitting the coefficients rather than moving the teeth.

\subsection{Metric definitions}
\label{sec:toy:metrics}

Every number in this work is one of the following, and I will never use
these words in any other sense:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
\item \textbf{segment efficiency} $\eff=n_{\rm true\,active}/n_{\rm true}$:
the fraction of true segments that survive selection;
\item \textbf{segment false rate}
$\far=n_{\rm false\,active}/n_{\rm active}$: the fraction of the
\emph{selected} sample that is fake (note the denominator: this is an
impurity, not a false-positive rate over all fakes);
\item \textbf{working point}: the \emph{efficiency-first working
point}, the lowest threshold that admits the true solutions: $\tau$
placed just below the 1\% quantile of the solver's own true-segment
amplitudes, so $\eff\ge0.99$ by construction. Whenever this work
quotes a false rate without qualification, this 99\% point is meant,
for every solver. Sections~\ref{sec:fork}--\ref{sec:fit} use the same
rule at other target efficiencies, there called
\emph{matched-efficiency}: $\tau$ just below the $k$-th largest true
amplitude with $k=\lceil\mathrm{eff}\cdot n_{\rm true}\rceil$, which
lets solvers be compared at identical efficiency without quantile-grid
artefacts. Because the threshold is a quantile of the solver's own
amplitudes, these metrics do not depend on the amplitude scale.
\item \textbf{AUC}, the threshold-free separation metric: sweeping
$\tau$ from high to low traces out a curve of efficiency against false
rate for each solver, and the \emph{area under that curve} summarises
how well the solver's amplitude ordering separates true from false
segments independently of any particular threshold choice. AUC~$=1$
means some threshold separates them perfectly; AUC~$=0.5$ means the
ordering is no better than chance. It is used when a single number for
``separation quality'' is needed, notably in
section~\ref{sec:occupancy}.
\end{itemize}
Quantum solutions are unit-norm vectors; before thresholding they are
rescaled to the classical amplitude scale on the classically-active
support. Rescaling by the full norm would be multiplicity-dependent,
since the false bulk owns most of the norm. All thresholds act on
$|x_i|$.

\subsection{The spectrum}
\label{sec:toy:spectrum}

Because $A=sI-C$, its spectrum is a rigid reflection of the
compatibility graph's: $\lambda(A)=s-\mu(C)$, where $\mu(C)$ denotes an
eigenvalue of $C$, the $\varepsilon$-windowed 0/1 compatibility
adjacency of section~\ref{sec:toy:ham} ($C_{ij}=1$ exactly when
segments $i$ and $j$ continue one another head-to-tail within the
angular acceptance $\varepsilon$, and $0$ otherwise). Every spectral
statement in this work is therefore a statement about the eigenvalues
of the small 0/1 graphs catalogued in section~\ref{sec:toy:graphs}, and
each of those families has a closed form:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item an \textbf{isolated segment}, $P_1$, gives $\lambda=s$ exactly. This
single line carries $\mathcal{O}(n_{\rm seg})$ modes: at $T=200$ under
heavy noise, $155{,}371$ of $157{,}609$ segments (rep~0).
\item a \textbf{chain} $P_m$ gives
$\lambda_k=s-2\cos\!\big(k\pi/(m{+}1)\big)$, $k=1,\dots,m$, so a
\emph{pair} $P_2$ sits at $s\mp1$ and a \emph{triple} $P_3$ at
$\{s,\,s\mp\sqrt2\}$. A true track ($m=4$) therefore owns a
four-dimensional block whose spectrum is exactly four lines,
\begin{equation}
\lambda\in\{s-2\cos(k\pi/5)\}_{k=1}^{4}
=\{2.382,\ 3.382,\ 4.618,\ 5.618\}\quad(s=4),
\label{eq:p4lines}
\end{equation}
symmetric about $s$ and split by the golden-ratio
cosines\footnote{The values $2\cos(\pi/5)=\varphi=1.618\dots$ and
$2\cos(2\pi/5)=1/\varphi$: the five-hit track is secretly a pentagon
problem.}.
\item a \textbf{hub} $K_{1,m}$ gives $\lambda=s\pm\sqrt m$ plus
$m{-}1$ copies of $s$ --- and, by section~\ref{sec:toy:modes}, only the two
outer lines are ever excited, however many continuations the hub has.
\end{enumerate}
Figure~\ref{fig:motifs} draws each of these motifs in hit space and
places its exact eigenvalue lines on a common $\lambda$-axis: this is
the dictionary between what a cluster of segments \emph{looks like} in
the detector and where its solution weight \emph{piles up} in the
spectrum. The pair motif at $\lambda=s\pm1$ and the triple at
$\{s,\,s\pm\sqrt2\}$ do most of the false-population work under noise.
It is tempting to say that their lines interleave the true lines of
eq.~\eqref{eq:p4lines} without touching them, and that is not quite true: the
triple has a line at exactly $\lambda=s$, and a hub $K_{1,m}$ has $m-1$ of
them, sitting squarely on the isolated line. What is true, and is what this
work exploits, is the statement of section~\ref{sec:toy:modes}: every false
family's \emph{bright} lines --- the ones the uniform bias can actually put
amplitude into --- lie close to but never on the true lines, while the lines
that do coincide are dark and carry nothing. When noise is switched on in
section~\ref{sec:noise}, the \emph{measured} spectral pile-up of a
heavy-noise event (figure~\ref{fig:pileup}) lands exactly on the lines
catalogued here. Motifs whose spectrum coincides
with a true motif's are the exception, and they return in
sections~\ref{sec:comb:limits} and~\ref{sec:fit} as the limiting factor.

\section{The one-bit quantum filter, read as a spectral filter}
\label{sec:onebit}

\subsection{The circuit, block by block}
\label{sec:onebit:circuit}

The three quantum algorithms of this lineage --- HHL, the one-bit quantum
filter of ref.~\cite{trackhhl}, and the QSVT developed from
section~\ref{sec:qsvt} onward --- are one family, and the cleanest way to
introduce the 1BQF precisely is to draw all three to the same conventions and
write down the state after every block (figure~\ref{fig:circuits}). Each acts
on a \emph{system} register of $n=\lceil\log_2 n_{\rm seg}\rceil$ qubits
holding one amplitude per segment, and each is read by post-selecting a flag.
What distinguishes them is what they do to an eigencomponent of $A$ between
preparation and readout.

\paragraph{HHL, in four blocks.} Write the input in the eigenbasis of $A$,
$|b\rangle=\sum_j\beta_j|u_j\rangle$ with $A|u_j\rangle=\lambda_j|u_j\rangle$.
Phase estimation with an $n_c$-qubit clock, driven by controlled powers of
$e^{iAt}$, writes an estimate $\tilde\lambda_j$ of each eigenvalue into the
clock, $|\Psi_2\rangle=\sum_j\beta_j|\tilde\lambda_j\rangle_T|u_j\rangle_S$;
a rotation of the flag conditioned on the clock value multiplies each
component by $C/\tilde\lambda_j$ on the flag's $|1\rangle$ branch,
$|\Psi_3\rangle=\sum_j\beta_j\big(\sqrt{1-C^2/\tilde\lambda_j^{2}}\,|0\rangle
+\tfrac{C}{\tilde\lambda_j}|1\rangle\big)_A|\tilde\lambda_j\rangle_T|u_j\rangle_S$;
the phase estimation is run backwards to return the clock to $|0\rangle$; and
post-selecting the flag leaves $|\Psi_4\rangle\propto\sum_j\beta_j
(C/\tilde\lambda_j)|u_j\rangle_S$, which is $C\,A^{-1}|b\rangle$ whenever the
clock resolves every eigenvalue exactly. Two costs follow from this
structure: $\approx2(2^{n_c}-1)$ calls to the controlled evolution for an
$n_c$-bit clock, and a success probability $\sum_j\beta_j^{2}C^{2}/
\tilde\lambda_j^{2}$ set by the smallest eigenvalue the rotation must
accommodate.

\paragraph{The 1BQF, in five blocks.} The one-bit filter is HHL with a
one-qubit clock and the rotation replaced by a flag. Its input is not
$|b\rangle$ but the uniform superposition, $|u\rangle=H^{\otimes n}|0\rangle$,
which is what $\mathbf{b}=\delta\mathbf{1}$ becomes once the register is padded
to a power of two with \emph{ones} (the padded rows of $A$ carry only the
diagonal $s$, so the padding modes sit at $\lambda=s$ and are dealt with
below). Writing $\beta_j=\langle u_j|u\rangle$ and abbreviating
$c_j=\cos(\lambda_jt/2)$, $s_j=\sin(\lambda_jt/2)$, the five states are
\begin{align}
|\Psi_1\rangle&=|0\rangle_A\,|{+}\rangle_T\sum_j\beta_j|u_j\rangle_S ,
\label{eq:psi1}\\
|\Psi_2\rangle&=|0\rangle_A\sum_j\beta_j\,e^{-i\lambda_jt/2}
\big(c_j|0\rangle_T+i\,s_j|1\rangle_T\big)|u_j\rangle_S ,
\label{eq:psi2}\\
|\Psi_3\rangle&=\sum_j\beta_j\,e^{-i\lambda_jt/2}
\big(c_j|1\rangle_A|0\rangle_T+i\,s_j|0\rangle_A|1\rangle_T\big)|u_j\rangle_S ,
\label{eq:psi3}\\
|\Psi_4\rangle&=\sum_j\beta_j\Big\{|1\rangle_A\big(c_j^{2}|0\rangle_T-i\,c_js_j|1\rangle_T\big)
+|0\rangle_A\big(s_j^{2}|0\rangle_T+i\,s_jc_j|1\rangle_T\big)\Big\}|u_j\rangle_S .
\label{eq:psi4}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:psi2} is the whole trick: the single controlled
$e^{-iAt}$ kicks the phase $e^{-i\lambda_jt}$ back onto the clock, and the
second Hadamard --- which is the one-qubit inverse Fourier transform ---
turns that phase into the amplitudes $\cos$ and $\sin$ of half of it. A
one-bit clock cannot \emph{estimate} an eigenvalue; it can only weigh each
eigencomponent by $\cos(\lambda_jt/2)$. The flag (eq.~\ref{eq:psi3}) is a
NOT on the ancilla conditioned on the clock reading $|0\rangle$, so it marks
the cosine branch. The uncompute (eq.~\ref{eq:psi4}) runs the one-bit phase
estimation backwards --- second Hadamard, the inverse evolution $e^{+iAt}$,
first Hadamard --- and the mode-dependent phases $e^{\mp i\lambda_jt/2}$
cancel exactly. What it does \emph{not} do is return the clock to
$|0\rangle$: because the flag acted in between, the clock stays entangled
with the flag, and the predecessors' statement that uncomputation
``restores the time register'' holds only in the binary idealisation in
which every $c_j$ is $0$ or $1$. Post-selecting the flag on $|1\rangle$
and tracing out the clock, the probability of reading segment $i$ is
therefore a sum of two squares,
\begin{equation}
p_i=\Big[\cos^{2}\!\big(\tfrac{At}{2}\big)\,\mathbf{u}\Big]_i^{2}
+\Big[\tfrac12\sin(At)\,\mathbf{u}\Big]_i^{2},
\qquad
P_{\rm anc}=\sum_i p_i=\sum_j\beta_j^{2}\cos^{2}\!\big(\tfrac{\lambda_jt}{2}\big),
\label{eq:onebitreadout}
\end{equation}
and the device's amplitude for segment $i$ is $x^{Q}_i=\sqrt{p_i}$, with the
sign of the underlying amplitude not recovered. The kept weight per
eigenmode is $c_j^{4}+c_j^{2}s_j^{2}=c_j^{2}$, so the success probability is
exactly the one the predecessors quote; but the \emph{pattern} of amplitude
over the segments is not $|\cos(At/2)\mathbf{u}|$, because the two clock
branches of eq.~\eqref{eq:psi4} add in probability rather than in amplitude.
Every filtered level quoted in this work is eq.~\eqref{eq:onebitreadout}
evaluated exactly; the difference from the single-cosine idealisation is a
few percent on a five-hit track and zero on anything the notch annihilates.
(The sign convention is immaterial: the circuit as built applies
$e^{-iAt}$, the predecessors write $e^{+iAt}$, and the cosine is even.)

\paragraph{The QSVT, in three blocks.} Its circuit is drawn in the same
figure for comparison and constructed in section~\ref{sec:qsvt}: prepare an
index register in $\sum_k\sqrt{|c_k|}\,|k\rangle$, apply the qubitization
walk $W$ a number of times controlled on the index, unprepare, and
post-select index and dilation qubit on zero, which leaves
$p(X)|u\rangle$ for the Chebyshev series $p=\sum_kc_kT_k$. Where the 1BQF's
clock can weigh a mode by one cosine, the walk's $k$-th power weighs it by
$T_k(x)=\cos(k\arccos x)$, and the index register sums those weights with
chosen coefficients. The one-bit filter is the $k=1$ term of that sum, and
the accounting of this whole work rests on the difference between owning one
term and owning $d$ of them.

\paragraph{Inside the two preparations.} The three circuits contain exactly
two state preparations between them, and figure~\ref{fig:prep} draws both.
The system register's is trivial and exact: $\mathbf{b}=\delta\mathbf{1}$ is
uniform, so $|u\rangle=H^{\otimes n}|0\rangle$ is one layer of $n$ Hadamards.
The input-loading problem that shadows quantum linear solvers in general never
arises on the register that carries the segments; it is displaced entirely
onto the index register, and that displacement is worth pricing, because it is
where the QSVT's extra freedom is bought. PREP must load the $d+1=41$
Chebyshev coefficients of the response onto $m=\lceil\log_2 41\rceil=6$
qubits,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PREP}\,|0\rangle^{\otimes m}=\sum_{k=0}^{2^{m}-1}
\sqrt{\frac{|c_k|}{\lVert c\rVert_1}}\,|k\rangle ,
\qquad
\lVert c\rVert_1=0.5144,\quad \max_k|c_k|=0.0466 ,
\label{eq:prep}
\end{equation}
and it does so as a binary cascade: one uniformly controlled $R_y$ per qubit,
the rotation acting on qubit $j$ selected by the $j$ bits already written.
Write $A(v)$ for the norm of the coefficients lying under node $v$ of that
binary tree, so that $A(v)=\sqrt{A(v_{\rm L})^{2}+A(v_{\rm R})^{2}}$ with the
leaves carrying $\pm\sqrt{|c_k|/\lVert c\rVert_1}$. Then the state after
level $j$ is the $(j{+}1)$-th row of the tree, and the angle that splits a
node is the angle between its two children:
\begin{equation}
|\Phi^{(j)}\rangle=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{j+1}}A(x)\,|x\rangle\,
|0\rangle^{\otimes(m-j-1)} ,
\qquad
\theta_v=2\arctan\frac{A(v_{\rm R})}{A(v_{\rm L})} ,
\label{eq:preptree}
\end{equation}
the arctangent taken in the full circle. Three things follow, and each is a
check on the cost of the build.

First, the signs are free. The tree has $2^{m}-1=63$ angles against $2^{m}=64$
amplitudes --- exactly the count that fixes a real vector up to normalisation,
signs and all --- so the same cascade with angles allowed outside $[0,\pi]$
loads the coefficients with their signs at identical cost. The $15$ negative
$c_k$ of the comb therefore need no diagonal of their own: the first
preparation loads the signed vector and the second the magnitudes, and
$\langle0|\mathrm{PREP}^{\dagger}\,\mathrm{SELECT}\,\mathrm{PREP}'|0\rangle=
\sum_kc_kT_k(X)/\lVert c\rVert_1$ as required. This is the only sense in which
the two boxes of figure~\ref{fig:circuits}(c) are not each other's inverse:
same cascade, different angles. The alternative, a $\pm1$ diagonal on the
index register, is far worse --- the comb's sign pattern has $36$ terms in its
algebraic normal form, up to a six-controlled $Z$.

Second, the register is mostly empty. Only $41$ of the $64$ index states carry
a coefficient, so $19$ of the $63$ angles sit under a branch of zero weight
and are dropped, leaving $44$. The states $|41\rangle$--$|63\rangle$ are never
populated, which is why the binary \textsc{select} may be allowed to execute
walk powers all the way to $W^{63}$: those branches carry no amplitude, so the
$23$ surplus powers cost depth and nothing else.

Third, and the reason for pricing it at all: the preparation does not scale
with the event. A dense loader on $m$ qubits costs at most $2^{m}-1=63$
rotations and $2^{m}-2=62$ CNOTs, and a compiler pass on the comb's actual
coefficient vector returns $57$ CX at depth $214$ ($188$ with the signs). One
call to the block encoding costs $1.65\times10^{4}$ two-qubit gates at
$T=400$ and the circuit makes $63$ of them, so the whole preparation is
$0.4\%$ of a single call and $6\times10^{-5}$ of the shot --- and it stays
$63$ rotations at $T=1000$, where a call costs $4.0\times10^{4}$. The index
register is where the polynomial lives, and loading it is not what the
polynomial costs.

\subsection{The response function}
\label{sec:onebit:response}

Reading eq.~\eqref{eq:onebitreadout} mode by mode, the whole device is
summarised by one real function of the eigenvalue, the kept amplitude
\begin{equation}
f(\lambda)=\cos\!\Big(\frac{\lambda t}{2}\Big),\qquad t=\frac{\pi}{s},
\label{eq:notch}
\end{equation}
and the choice $t=\pi/s$ places its zero exactly at $\lambda=s$, the
isolated-segment line. On that line $c_j=0$, so both terms of
eq.~\eqref{eq:onebitreadout} vanish: every isolated false segment, all
$\mathcal{O}(n_{\rm seg})$ of them, is annihilated --- not suppressed,
killed --- and the padding modes, which sit on the same line, go with them.
This is the entire content of the 1BQF, and the form in which it will be
needed later is an accounting rule: a degree-one response owns exactly one
zero, and the 1BQF spends it on the largest false population in the problem.
Figure~\ref{fig:responses} draws the geometry. The single zero sits on the
isolated line, and every other bright line of the false families of
section~\ref{sec:toy:graphs} --- the pair at $s\pm1$, the triple at
$s\pm\sqrt2$, the hub at $s\pm\sqrt m$ --- lands on the slopes of the cosine
and passes with large weight: $f(3)=0.38$ for a pair, $f(2.59)=0.53$ for a
triple, against $f(2.38)=0.59$ for a true track's dominant mode. The filter
attacks the isolated segments and nothing else; whatever false weight is
\emph{coupled} sails through. The lower panel shows the alternative that
the rest of this work builds: a degree-40 polynomial with narrow passes at
the true-track lines and effective zeros everywhere else, the isolated, pair
and triple lines included.

Figure~\ref{fig:notchspec} makes the accounting rule quantitative, on two
real events. The kept probability of eq.~\eqref{eq:onebitreadout}
decomposes exactly over the eigenmodes of $A$, and each mode's weight splits
by its eigenvector into a true share and a false share; histogramming the
false share against $\lambda$ shows where the errors live before and after
the filter. Before it --- under the classical inversion, whose per-mode
weight is $\beta_j^{2}/\lambda_j^{2}$ --- the false weight is one spike:
$99.6\%$ of it sits on the single line $\lambda=s$ on the clean $T=400$
event, $98.1\%$ on the heavy-noise $T=200$ event, and the false-to-true
weight ratio is $144$ to $1$ and $61$ to $1$. After it that spike is exactly
zero ($10^{-33}$, the floating-point residue of $\cos(\pi/2)$), the ratio has
fallen to $0.36$ and $0.87$ to $1$, and what survives is entirely the coupled
population the notch cannot reach: on the clean event $94\%$ of the surviving
false weight is on the pair lines $s\pm1$ and $3\%$ on the triple lines; under
heavy noise $73\%$ pairs, $15\%$ triples, $1\%$ hubs, $8\%$ spread over the
continuum between the lines that noise creates, and $2\%$ sitting \emph{on
the true lines} --- the spectral twins of section~\ref{sec:comb:limits},
which no function of $\lambda$ will ever separate. One zero removes one
line; every line that remains needs a zero of its own.

One statement about the circuit as built belongs here rather than later.
The package realises $e^{-iAt}$ as a product of one two-level rotation per
compatible pair (the direct structural synthesis of ref.~\cite{trackhhl}),
which is exact when the pairs commute and a product-formula approximation
when they share a segment; section~\ref{sec:fork:trotter} measures the
agreement with exact evolution at cosine $\approx0.985$ on the uniform-weight
Hamiltonian of this work. Everything analytic in this section is the exact
circuit of figure~\ref{fig:circuits}b; every curve read from the solution
store (figures~\ref{fig:threshold} and~\ref{fig:clean2x2}) is the circuit as
built, and is labelled so.


\subsection{What the notch does to a track, and what it lets through}
\label{sec:onebit:track}

Away from its zero the cosine is not flat, and the first consequence is
that true amplitudes are reshaped. Section~\ref{sec:toy:modes} showed that
a true track's solution lives on two bright modes, at $\lambda=2.382$ and
$4.618$, and the notch at $s=4$ sits \emph{between} them: the response is
$+0.593$ on the first and $-0.240$ on the second. The filter therefore
flips the sign of the smaller mode. On the two end segments, where the two
modes had reinforced one another, they now partly cancel; on the two middle
segments, where they had opposed, they now add. Evaluated with
eq.~\eqref{eq:onebitreadout} and placed on the classical scale by the atlas'
single global factor, the endpoints of a clean track fall from $4/11=0.364$
to $0.266$ while the middles rise from $5/11=0.455$ to $0.492$
(figures~\ref{fig:atlas} and~\ref{fig:ladder}). The endpoint amplitudes
remain perfectly well ordered above the false levels of a clean event, so
the efficiency-first threshold, which sits just below $0.266$, keeps every
segment of every complete track.

The second consequence is the one that decides this work, and it can be
written out on three clusters. A false pair --- two fake segments that
happen to continue one another inside the acceptance --- has its bright
line at $\lambda=s-1$, where $f=0.383$, and comes out of the filter at
$0.261$: five thousandths below the true endpoints, separable on a clean
event by the thinnest of margins and not at all once noise smears either
population. A false triple, bright at $s\pm\sqrt2$, comes out at $0.287$ on
its ends and $0.472$ at its centre, \emph{above} the true endpoints and
level with the true middles. A three-way hub $K_{1,3}$ comes out at $0.637$
at its centre, above every true segment in the event. The efficiency-first
threshold, pinned at $0.266$ by the true endpoints, must admit all of them.
Nothing here is a matter of tuning: the notch has one zero and it is already
spent, so a coupled false cluster is filtered by whatever value the cosine
happens to take at its bright lines, and those values are of the same order
as the true track's. This is, cluster by cluster, the false-rate curve of
figure~\ref{fig:clean2x2}: as density or acceptance grows, the coupled-false
population grows, and the 1BQF's false rate grows with it. The 1BQF is,
precisely, the statement that unconnected fakes are exactly removable;
everything in this work beyond section~\ref{sec:qsvt} is about the
connected ones.

five-hit track down to $0.266$ and the middles up to $0.492$, and annihilates
the one-segment stub outright, since a lone segment sits exactly on the
notch. The marked levels are what a threshold has to clear to keep every
segment of a complete track.}
\label{fig:ladder}
\end{figure}

\subsection{The threshold trade-off}
\label{sec:onebit:threshold}

Figure~\ref{fig:threshold} shows the whole trade explicitly, on a stored
$T=400$ clean event of the benchmark campaign, by sweeping the threshold for
all three solvers of this work with the quantum amplitudes norm-matched to
the classical solver on its own working-point support. The efficiency panel
is a staircase for every solver, because on a clean event the true segments

the exact circuit; that is a property of the package circuit's uncompute
step, which leaves the four segments of a track at four distinct levels
depending on their labelling order, and it is left as it is here.

\subsection{HHL and the one-bit filter, side by side}
\label{sec:onebit:hhl}

Nothing in this programme had measured HHL at the segment level, and the
comparison is worth having because the three algorithms of
figure~\ref{fig:circuits} sit on one axis: what each does to an
eigencomponent. HHL's action is a response like the others', $C/\tilde\lambda$
with $\tilde\lambda$ the clock's estimate of $\lambda$, so it can be
emulated exactly through the same per-cluster eigendecomposition as every
other curve in this work: the standard finite-clock phase-estimation kernel
with its leakage, the rotation keyed on the estimated clock value, the true
inverse of the phase estimation, and the clock traced out. The emulation
agrees with an exact circuit to $10^{-12}$ at small $T$.
Figure~\ref{fig:hhl1bqf} reads it on the events of figure~\ref{fig:clean2x2}
at the same efficiency-first working point, with the classical solver, the
stored 1BQF and the QSVT alongside, three events per multiplicity.

Two things are visible. With a clock of six or eight bits and the textbook
gain $C=\lambda_{\min}$, HHL reproduces the classical false rate to three
decimals at every multiplicity --- $1.60\%$ at $T=400$, $4.24\%$ at
$T=700$, $10.4\%$ at $T=1000$ --- because it \emph{is} the classical
inverse: an exact clock and
a $1/\tilde\lambda$ rotation return $A^{-1}\mathbf{b}$, isolated segments at
their $0.25$ attractor included. HHL does not null the isolated line. It
reproduces the haystack faithfully and leaves the threshold to sort it, at
$2(2^{n_c}-1)=126$ to $510$ evolution calls per shot against the 1BQF's two.
With four clock bits the resolution is too coarse: the isolated line at $s$
and the true lines share bins and leak into one another, and the false rate
rises to $16\%$ at $T=400$, $27\%$ at $T=700$ and $38\%$ at $T=1000$,
between the classical solver and the 1BQF. The gain fixed in the package implementation, finally,
saturates the rotation at every clock value for any system larger than a
few segments, so that circuit flags everything and its false rate is
$1-1/T$, the random-ordering value; that is why no HHL segment-level result
existed before this one, and it is left as a to-do.

The success probabilities complete the picture and are the thread into the
next section. HHL's kept probability is large --- $P_{\rm anc}\approx0.34$
at $T=100$ and $0.07$ at $T=1000$ for the eight-bit clock --- precisely
because it keeps the false bulk: every isolated segment is rotated by $C/s$
like everything else. The 1BQF's is $\approx0.2/T$ because the bulk has
already been thrown away before the flag is read. The one-bit filter's small
success probability is the price of doing the selection inside the circuit
rather than in the readout, and the QSVT pays a price of the same kind,
measured in section~\ref{sec:comb:resources}.

\subsection{Why one zero is not enough}
\label{sec:onebit:motivation}

On success probability first, because it is the currency in which the
next section's construction is paid. The kept-branch probability is
eq.~\eqref{eq:onebitreadout}: for an exact five-hit track the two bright
modes contribute $\cos^{2}$ weights of $0.352$ and $0.058$ to a per-track
total of $0.337$, spread over the $4T^{2}$ segments of the register, so on
clean events $P_{\rm anc}\approx0.34\,T/n_{\rm pad}$ with $n_{\rm pad}$ the
padded dimension --- $0.21/T$ where the padding ratio is $0.61$ and $0.32/T$
where it is $0.95$. Measured on the stored solutions the constant is $0.18$
to $0.25$ from $T=10$ to $550$ and then rises, to $0.60$ at $T=700$ and
$0.98$ at $T=1000$: the coupled false population that the notch lets
through adds kept weight of its own, so $P_{\rm anc}$ is itself a diagnostic
of the false pile-up. Shot budgets grow linearly in $T$, and amplitude
amplification buys the usual quadratic discount; these costs carry over
essentially unchanged to the polynomial filters below, which is why the
resource accounting is deferred to section~\ref{sec:comb:resources}.

The motivation for everything that follows is now a single sentence with
numbers behind it. A response of degree one owns one zero, and this
problem's false population has more than one line: the isolated line at
$s$, which the 1BQF kills; the pair lines at $s\pm1$, the triple lines at
$s\pm\sqrt2$, the hub lines at $s\pm\sqrt m$, which it passes at
$0.38$--$0.53$ of their weight; and under noise the tangles that
section~\ref{sec:noise} measures, spread across the whole band. Each of
those lines needs a zero of its own, and the true lines between them need
to be passed. A polynomial of degree $d$ owns $d$ zeros and can be shaped
between them, and section~\ref{sec:qsvt} shows that a quantum computer
applies such a polynomial to $A$ with the same primitive the 1BQF already
uses --- the controlled evolution, called $d$ times instead of two ---
without diagonalising anything. That is the QSVT, and the one-bit filter is
its first term.

\section{From one bit to any polynomial: the QSVT construction}
\label{sec:qsvt}

The one-bit filter weighs every eigencomponent by one cosine. Everything
from here on weighs it by a polynomial, and this section is the deep dive
into what that means: what the theorem behind it actually says, the three
circuits that realise it, how the polynomial is found, and what it costs
--- classically and on a quantum computer --- with every number measured or
modelled on the same events as the rest of the paper.
Readers who trust the QSVT
literature~\cite{gslw2019,lowchuang2019,childs2012,martyn2021} can skim to
the cost model of section~\ref{sec:qsvt:resources}; the details are
included because every resource claim in this work is read directly off
these constructions.

\subsection{What a polynomial of a matrix buys, and what QSVT is}
\label{sec:qsvt:what}

A function $f$ of the Hamiltonian acts mode by mode, $f(A)\mathbf{u}=
\sum_j f(\lambda_j)\beta_j|u_j\rangle$, so choosing $f$ is choosing a
weight for every eigenvalue. A polynomial of degree $d$ has $d$ roots to
place, and it is the class of functions a quantum circuit can apply with a
number of operator calls equal to the degree. The theorem that says so is
the quantum singular value transformation~\cite{gslw2019}, which in the
Hermitian form this work needs reads as follows. Rescale the Hamiltonian
affinely so that its spectrum fits in $[-1,1]$: $X=(A-c_0I)/c_1$, with
$c_0$ the centre of the polynomial's domain and $c_1$ its
half-width\footnote{The implementation pads the estimated spectral bounds
by a few percent; an early campaign bug taught us that the polynomial's
domain must contain the padded spectrum or the constructor rightly
refuses.}. A \emph{block encoding} of $X$ is a unitary $U$ on the system
register plus one more qubit whose top-left block is $X$,
\begin{equation}
(\langle0|\otimes I)\,U\,(|0\rangle\otimes I)=X,
\qquad\text{for instance}\qquad
U=\begin{pmatrix} X & \sqrt{I-X^{2}}\\[2pt] \sqrt{I-X^{2}} & -X
\end{pmatrix},
\label{eq:dilation}
\end{equation}
the \emph{dilation}, which is the block encoding the simulations use.
Because $X$ is symmetric, $U$ decomposes over the eigenvectors of $X$: for
every eigenvalue $x$ there is a two-dimensional invariant plane, spanned by
$|u_j\rangle$ with the extra qubit in $|0\rangle$ and in $|1\rangle$, in
which $U$ acts as the reflection
\begin{equation}
R(x)=\begin{pmatrix} x & \sqrt{1-x^{2}}\\ \sqrt{1-x^{2}} & -x\end{pmatrix}.
\label{eq:eigenplane}
\end{equation}
Now let $\Pi$ be the projector onto ``extra qubit in $|0\rangle$'' and
$e^{i\phi(2\Pi-I)}$ the rotation that phases the two halves of every
eigenplane oppositely --- in each plane it is $e^{i\phi Z}$. The theorem:
\begin{quote}
\emph{For every real polynomial $q$ of degree $d$ with definite parity,
$q(-x)=\pm q(x)$, and $|q(x)|\le1$ on $[-1,1]$, there exist phases
$\phi_0,\dots,\phi_d$ such that the circuit}
\begin{equation}
U_{\Phi}=e^{i\phi_0(2\Pi-I)}\prod_{k=1}^{d}\Big[U^{(\dagger)}\,e^{i\phi_k(2\Pi-I)}\Big],
\label{eq:qsvt}
\end{equation}
\emph{with $U$ and $U^{\dagger}$ alternating, satisfies
$(\langle0|\otimes I)\,U_{\Phi}\,(|0\rangle\otimes I)=P(X)$ for a
polynomial $P$ whose real part is $q$. It uses $U$ exactly $d$ times, one
signal qubit for the phases, and the phases are found classically.}
\end{quote}
The whole content is the eigenplane picture: eq.~\eqref{eq:qsvt}
restricted to one plane is a product of $2\times2$ matrices,
$e^{i\phi_0Z}\prod_k R(x)e^{i\phi_kZ}$, whose top-left element is a
polynomial in $x$ of degree $d$ --- quantum signal processing on one qubit
--- and the existence of phases for any bounded definite-parity $q$ is a
statement about such products~\cite{lowchuang2019,gslw2019}. Applying it
in every eigenplane at once, which is what $U$ does, applies $q$ to every
eigenvalue at once, without diagonalising anything. The imaginary part of
$P$ is the price of unitarity and is discarded by the standard real-part
construction (one more ancilla holding the sequence and its conjugate in
superposition, section~\ref{sec:qsvt:realisations}); the parity condition
is real, and is met by splitting a general polynomial into its even and odd
parts.

Two special phase choices are worth naming because the rest of this work is
built on them. With every phase zero, the product of reflections is not a
polynomial one wants; but the \emph{qubitization walk}
$W=(Z\otimes I)\,U$, which turns each reflection $R(x)$ into a rotation by
$\arccos x$, has the defining property
\begin{equation}
\big\langle 0\big|\,W^{k}\,\big|0\big\rangle_{\rm dil}=T_{k}(X),
\label{eq:walkcheb}
\end{equation}
where $T_k(x)=\cos(k\arccos x)$ is the $k$-th Chebyshev polynomial: walking
$k$ steps and reading the undeflected component applies $T_k$, exactly,
with no error term and every operator real. A weighted sum of walk powers
is then any polynomial, and this is the realisation the simulations run.
And the one-bit filter of section~\ref{sec:onebit} is the degree-one
member of the same family on a different primitive: the evolution
$e^{-iAt}$ in place of the block encoding, and one cosine in place of one
Chebyshev polynomial. Chebyshev polynomials of the block encoding and
cosines of the evolution are two bases for the same object --- a bounded
polynomial response on the spectrum --- and the three circuits below are
three ways of assembling it.

\subsection{Three ways to apply it}
\label{sec:qsvt:realisations}

The response is one thing; the circuit that applies it is a choice, and
the choice sets the width, the depth and the success probability. Three
realisations appear in this work, drawn in figure~\ref{fig:circuits}(c)
and figure~\ref{fig:circuitsqsvt}, and table~\ref{tab:realisations}
collects what each costs at the production degree $d=40$.

\paragraph{The linear combination of Chebyshev powers (the build).} With
the walk of eq.~\eqref{eq:walkcheb}, a response $p(X)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}c_kT_k(X)$
is a weighted sum of walk powers. Introduce an index register of
$m=\lceil\log_2(d{+}1)\rceil$ qubits, prepare it in
$\sum_k\sqrt{|c_k|/\lVert c\rVert_1}\,|k\rangle$ with the coefficient signs
carried by the cascade itself (figure~\ref{fig:prep}), apply $W^{k}$
controlled on the index --- as built,
controlled powers $W^{2^{j}}$ from index qubit $j$, so the circuit executes
$2^{m}-1=63$ walk steps at $d=40$ where a unary SELECT would execute $d$ ---
undo the preparation with the unsigned angles, and post-select index and
dilation on zero. The
surviving state is $\propto p(X)|u\rangle$ with success probability
\begin{equation}
P_{\rm anc}=\frac{\big\lVert p(X)\,|u\rangle\big\rVert^{2}}
{\big(\sum_k |c_k|\big)^{2}} ,
\label{eq:psucc}
\end{equation}
the usual price of a linear combination of unitaries: the one-norm of the
coefficients. No phases are needed and every operator is real, which is
why this is the form the exact simulations use. Its cost is width ---
$1+m=7$ ancillas at $d=40$ --- and controlled walks.

\paragraph{The phase sequence (QSVT proper).} Equation~\eqref{eq:qsvt}
directly: $d$ uncontrolled applications of $U$, one signal qubit, and the
polynomial itself rather than its one-norm in the success probability.
This work has now built it for the production comb. The comb has no
definite parity, so it is split, $p=p_{\rm e}+p_{\rm o}$, each part
rescaled to peak at one, $q_{\rm e}=p_{\rm e}/\alpha_{\rm e}$ and
$q_{\rm o}=p_{\rm o}/\alpha_{\rm o}$, and given its own sequence: $41$
phases for the even part and $40$ for the odd, found by the symmetric-phase
method of ref.~\cite{dong2021} (a quasi-Newton minimisation of the mismatch
on Chebyshev nodes; $7$\,s and $171$\,s from a cold start) to a scalar
accuracy of $2\times10^{-7}$ and $8\times10^{-7}$. The circuit was then
run as dense linear algebra on a $36$-segment event --- the block encoding
of eq.~\eqref{eq:dilation}, the projector-controlled rotations, $40$
applications --- and reproduces $q(X)|u\rangle$ on the real part of the
post-selected branch to $2\times10^{-9}$ (even) and $2\times10^{-7}$ (odd).
Two honest additions to the textbook count follow. The real part costs one
ancilla and the sequence together with its conjugate in superposition; the
parity split costs one more ancilla and both sequences. So a real,
indefinite-parity polynomial such as the comb runs on $n_{\rm sys}+4$
qubits with $4d$ block-encoding calls per shot ($2d$ for a polynomial of
definite parity, which the even-height four-tooth comb of
section~\ref{sec:toy:modes} would be), and its success probability is
\begin{equation}
P_{\rm anc}=\frac{\lVert q(X)|u\rangle\rVert^{2}}{(\alpha_{\rm e}+\alpha_{\rm o})^{2}},
\qquad q=p/\max|p|,\quad
(\alpha_{\rm e}+\alpha_{\rm o})^{2}=1.001 .
\label{eq:psucc_phase}
\end{equation}
The comb's even and odd peaks happen to add to almost exactly one, so the
split costs nothing in probability; and because the polynomial may be
scaled to $\max|p|=1$ while the one-norm route may not, the phase sequence
gains a factor $(\lVert c\rVert_1/\max|p|)^{2}=(0.514/0.349)^{2}=2.2$ over
the build in success probability (figure~\ref{fig:combdeg}c), which is
precisely the LCU penalty.

\paragraph{The trigonometric comb over the evolution (QETU).} The one-bit
filter's primitive is $e^{-iAt}$, synthesised as one two-level rotation per
compatible pair, and the same primitive can carry the comb: a
trigonometric polynomial $\sum_{k=0}^{K}c_k\cos(kAt)$ applied by the
quantum eigenvalue transformation of unitaries~\cite{dong2022} --- $K$
controlled evolutions interleaved with single-qubit rotations on one
ancilla, figure~\ref{fig:circuitsqsvt}(b). It runs at exactly the one-bit
filter's width, $n_{\rm sys}+2$, and is the hardware route of
section~\ref{sec:limits:resources}. A comb of order $K=40$ in
$\theta=\lambda t$ with $t=\pi/7.9$ reproduces the production comb's
segment metrics on the clean events; its rotations are not synthesised in
this work (the resource numbers do not depend on them), and, being built on
the per-pair product for $e^{-iAt}$, it inherits the product-formula
caveat of section~\ref{sec:fork:trotter}.

\subsection{Finding the polynomial}
\label{sec:qsvt:design}

The polynomial is found in three steps and constrained by two rules, and
figure~\ref{fig:combdeg} shows all of it on the production comb.

\emph{Target.} The response wanted is written down as a function of
$\lambda$ on the polynomial's domain, here $[0.2,\,7.8]$. For the comb it
is a Gaussian pass of half-width $h_w=0.18$ at each of the four true-track
lines $s-2\cos(k\pi/5)$, weighted by $1/\lambda$ so that the passed
amplitudes reproduce the classical profile inside a track, and zero
elsewhere; the retired first design was a contiguous band with notches
(section~\ref{sec:comb}). \emph{Fit.} The target is sampled on a grid of
$6000$ points and fitted by a Chebyshev series of degree $d$ in least
squares, which is a linear problem solved in milliseconds. The minimax
alternative --- the same target fitted in the Chebyshev norm by a linear
program --- equiripples the error and reaches a smaller worst-case
deviation at the same degree ($0.16$ against $0.26$ at $d=16$) at the
price of spreading it everywhere (figure~\ref{fig:combdeg}a); the
production design is the least-squares one. \emph{Bound.} A response is
realisable only if $|p|\le1$ on the domain, so the series is rescaled;
the comb as fitted peaks at $0.349$, which the phase sequence scales up to
one and the one-norm route cannot. Section~\ref{sec:fit} replaces the first
step by a measurement --- the response's binned values become the unknowns
of a quadratic objective on the observed pile-up --- and keeps the other two.

The two rules are what set the degree. The first is the \emph{resolution
law}: a Chebyshev series of degree $d$ on a domain of half-width $W$ can
resolve no feature narrower than about $2W/d$. With $W=3.8$ that is $0.19$
at $d=40$ --- just enough to separate the triple line at $s-\sqrt2$ from
the nearest true line, $0.20$ away, and to keep a pass of full width $0.36$
from spilling into its neighbour --- and $0.48$ at $d=16$, where the passes
have merged into a band that still rejects the isolated line and the
pairs, which is why the half-resolved comb of section~\ref{sec:comb:resources}
holds the metrics to $T=700$ at a fraction of the depth
(figure~\ref{fig:combdeg}d). Between the two, intermediate degrees can land
on unlucky fits whose false bulk swallows the true tail, the cliffs of
section~\ref{sec:comb:resources}; degrees are validated, not
interpolated. The second rule is \emph{parity}: the phase sequence needs
$q(-x)=\pm q(x)$, the comb has both parts, and the split of
section~\ref{sec:qsvt:realisations} is the price --- unless the target is
designed even from the start, which an equal-height comb on the four lines
would be.

\subsection{Classical cost}
\label{sec:qsvt:classical}

Everything above can be run classically, and it is worth being exact about
what that costs, because the answer is not the one a quantum-advantage
narrative would like. The design is cheap: the least-squares fit is
milliseconds, the minimax program seconds, and the phases of the sequence
are found once per design in minutes. The pre-step that is not cheap is
the spectral interval: the polynomial's domain must contain the spectrum,
and estimating $\lambda_{\min}$ and $\lambda_{\max}$ by a Lanczos iteration
on the event's matrix is the slowest classical step of the pipeline.
Applying the polynomial is the Chebyshev three-term recursion on sparse
matrix--vector products, $\mathcal{O}(d\cdot{\rm nnz}(A))$ flops, and the
evaluator agrees with the full circuit to better than $10^{-14}$ wherever
the circuit can be simulated.

The comparison that matters is with the classical solver it replaces.
Table~\ref{tab:classicalcost} measures both on the figure-\ref{fig:clean2x2}
events. MINRES converges to $10^{-8}$ in four to nine iterations at every
multiplicity, because $A$ is nearly diagonal --- ${\rm nnz}(A)$ exceeds
$n_{\rm seg}$ by well under a percent --- so the classical inverse costs
four to ten times \emph{fewer} matrix--vector products than the degree-40
polynomial. The polynomial is not a faster classical solver; it is a
different response, and on a classical machine the whole of this work is
spectral-filter engineering at a cost of seconds per event. The quantum
claim is confined to the resource profile of the next subsection.

\subsection{Quantum cost}
\label{sec:qsvt:resources}

The quantum cost model has five factors, and every later resource
statement in this work is one of them restated: width, calls per shot,
gates per call, amplification, readout. Figure~\ref{fig:resmodel} draws
the first four against track count for HHL, the one-bit filter and the
three realisations of the comb, with fitted power laws, and
table~\ref{tab:worked} works the whole chain through at $T=400$ and
$T=1000$.

\emph{Width} is the system register $n_{\rm sys}=\lceil\log_2 4T^{2}\rceil$
--- $16$ qubits at $T=100$, $20$ at $400$, $22$ at $1000$ --- plus a
constant that is the whole difference between the algorithms: $+2$ for the
one-bit filter and for the comb over $e^{-iAt}$, $+4$ for the phase
sequence, $+7$ for the one-norm build at $d=40$, and $+n_c+1$ for HHL,
$+9$ at eight clock bits. Every route fits an LHCb-multiplicity event in
$24$ to $31$ logical qubits; the block-encoding routes can trade width for
depth by adding the $45$-qubit workspace of the hit-priced encoding below.

Figure~\ref{fig:qubitscaling} draws the width and the depth in the
presentation of the predecessor papers~\cite{trackhhl_epj,trackhhl}: against
the matrix size $N=4T^{2}$, with the HL-LHC-scale event annotated. The
predecessors' headline --- HHL's $2\lceil\log_2N\rceil+2$ against the
one-bit filter's $\lceil\log_2N\rceil+2$, $50$ against $26$ qubits at
$T=1500$ --- is the top and bottom curve of panel (a); the comb sits between
them, at the one-bit filter's own width when it runs on $e^{-iAt}$ and two to
five qubits above it on a block encoding, plus the workspace if that block
encoding is priced by hits. Panel (b) is the depth: two-qubit gates per
shot, which is the calls of table~\ref{tab:realisations} times the two
per-call prices.

\emph{Calls per shot} are independent of $T$: $2$, $40$, $63$, $160$ and
$510$ for the 1BQF, the trigonometric comb, the one-norm build, the phase
sequence and eight-bit HHL respectively (table~\ref{tab:realisations}).

\emph{Gates per call} depend on how $A$ enters, and there are two prices.
The evolution $e^{-iAt}$ as a per-pair product costs one two-level rotation
per compatible pair, measured at $3.6\times10^{3}$ CX at $T=10$ rising as
$T^{1.37}$ to $6.2\times10^{5}$ at $T=400$ ($2.0\times10^{6}$ at $T=1000$
by extrapolation); this is the price of the 1BQF, of HHL and of the
trigonometric comb. A block encoding of $A$ priced per matrix entry costs
the same order (the dense dilation of the simulations is not a deployment
circuit at all: $4^{n_{\rm sys}}$ elements). The block encoding that
queries \emph{hits} instead of entries (section~\ref{sec:limits:blockencoding})
is modelled at $103\,T^{0.85}$ CX per call --- $1.6\times10^{4}$ at
$T=400$, $4.0\times10^{4}$ at $T=1000$ --- for $45$ workspace qubits, and
is the price used here for the two block-encoding realisations.

\emph{Amplification.} The post-selection succeeds with the probabilities of
table~\ref{tab:realisations}, all measured or computed on the stored events
and all $\propto1/T$ for the comb, because the uniform input spreads over
$4T^{2}$ segments while $\mathcal{O}(T)$ carry surviving amplitude:
$0.43/T$ for the one-norm build (the store's measurement, reproduced to
three digits by the matrix-free norm), $0.95/T$ for the phase sequence and
$0.96/T$ for the trigonometric comb, against $0.09\,T^{-0.79}$ for the
one-bit filter, whose constant rises at high multiplicity as coupled false
segments add kept weight, and $1.6\,T^{-0.40}$ for eight-bit HHL, which
keeps the false bulk and pays for it at readout instead. Amplitude
amplification turns $1/P$ repetitions into $\pi/(4\sqrt{P})$, so the calls
per delivered sample are calls per shot times that: at $T=400$, $63$ for the
1BQF, $641$ for the trigonometric comb, $1504$ for the one-norm build,
$2585$ for the phase sequence and $1116$ for eight-bit HHL.

\emph{Readout.} A delivered sample is one segment index drawn from the
filtered distribution; seeing each of the $M\approx4T$ surviving true
segments needs $M\ln M$ successes, $1.2\times10^{4}$ at $T=400$ and
$3.3\times10^{4}$ at $T=1000$, and the full-event budget is that times the
calls per delivered sample: the $d\,T^{1.5}\log T$ walk-call scaling that
section~\ref{sec:limits:readout} compares with the classical
$d\,T^{2}$ flop budget --- a square-root-class separation for the readout
task, and no more.

success probability, in amplification; section~\ref{sec:comb} shows how
much degree the physics actually needs.

\section{Fitting the polynomial to the clean events: the line comb}
\label{sec:comb}

\subsection{The design insight: the true spectrum is discrete}

Here is the observation that turns QSVT from a mathematical
possibility into a design tool. On this detector geometry every true
track has exactly five hits, hence exactly four segments, hence, by
eq.~\eqref{eq:p4lines}, exactly the same four eigenvalues. The true
spectrum of the entire event, however many thousand tracks it
contains, is four discrete lines. We are not trying to pass a region
of the spectrum; we are trying to pass four known radio stations, and
everything between them is static.

My first design did not understand this. A band-limited approximate
inverse ($\approx1/\lambda$ across a contiguous band containing the
true lines, zero outside) works beautifully at low multiplicity and
then fails exactly where it matters: as $T$ grows, accidental tangles
of false segments produce eigenvalues throughout the band, and a band
design passes them all. Its false rate reached tens of percent by
$T=400$, worse than classical.\footnote{A useful
failure: it taught us that the exploitable structure is the
discreteness of the geometry-pinned true spectrum, not its location.}
The production design is therefore a \emph{line comb}: narrow Gaussian
passes of half-width $h_w$, weighted $\propto1/\lambda$, centred at
the four lines $s-2\cos(k\pi/5)$ only, fitted as a bounded Chebyshev
series of degree $d$. The defaults, fixed once by a two-dimensional
$(d,h_w)$ scan and never tuned per event, are $d=40$, $h_w=0.18$.
This degree-40 line comb on the base Hamiltonian $A$ is \emph{the
QSVT} of this work, the comb, so called because it combs the false
tracks out of the spectrum: wherever the text says ``the QSVT'' or
``the comb'' without qualification, this polynomial on $A$ is meant.
Every other QSVT result is labelled by its polynomial degree and by
the Hamiltonian it is set up on, and where a polynomial of the same
degree has its coefficients refit to the events
(section~\ref{sec:fit}) the text says so.

Why does a width of $0.18$ work? The nearest systematically-populated
false lines, the $P_3$ bridge modes at $\{s-\sqrt2,\,s,\,s+\sqrt2\}$,
sit $\gtrsim0.2$ away from the true lines, so the comb threads the
gap; and the resolution law of a degree-$d$ Chebyshev series, features
no narrower than $\approx 2W/d$ on a domain of half-width $W$, makes
$d=40$ sufficient for $h_w=0.18$ on the clean-event domain. Degree,
width, and line spacing are one budget, and this is the budget the
noisy events of section~\ref{sec:fit} will re-open.

\subsection{Clean-event results}
\label{sec:comb:results}

On the fixed-acceptance ($\varepsilon=2$\,mrad), $\gamma=3$ study, the
canonical benchmark of the programme, with events and solutions shared
segment-for-segment across all three solvers through a common data
pipeline, every solver read at the efficiency-first working point of
section~\ref{sec:toy:metrics} ($99\%$ of its true segments kept),
the picture is:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
\item \textbf{Classical}: efficiency $100\%$ throughout (the true
amplitudes are tightly clustered, so the threshold that keeps $99\%$
keeps them all); false rate grows from $\approx0$ at low $T$ to
$0.65\%$ at $T=200$, $1.49\%$ at $T=400$, $4.9\%$ at $T=700$ and
$10.0\%$ at $T=1000$ as the $\mathcal{O}(T^2)$ false pairing feeds
the active set.
\item \textbf{1BQF}: the notch recovers essentially every true
segment (efficiency $99.5$--$100\%$ at every $T$), and the false rate
blows up to pay for it: $5.1\%$ at $T=100$, $43.9\%$ at $T=400$,
$82.6\%$ at $T=1000$, far above the classical solver, because the
single zero removes only the isolated-false bulk while every coupled
fake sits off the notch and survives.
\item \textbf{QSVT}: efficiency $99.1$--$100\%$ with a false rate of
$0.31\%$ at $T=200$ against the classical $0.65\%$ (zero to a few
false actives per event where the classical solver admits tens), a
tie at $T=400$ ($1.44\%$ against $1.49\%$), and then behind:
$9.1\%$ at $T=700$ and $20.7\%$ at $T=1000$ against the classical
$4.9\%$ and $10.0\%$.
\end{itemize}

The comb wins clearly through $T=300$ and ties at $T=400$; beyond
that, at this acceptance, it loses. Two things happen at once. The
composition effect dominates everyone: the false-to-true ratio
$n_F/n_T\approx T-1$ converts even excellent per-segment separation
into a growing impurity. And the clean-event design itself stops
being right: from $T\approx400$ the $2$\,mrad events develop tangles
of false segments whose eigenvalues enter the comb's passes
(section~\ref{sec:comb:limits}), so a polynomial pinned to the
isolated-chain lines no longer separates. The remedy is not a
different solver but the same degree-40 polynomial with its
coefficients refit to the events, the subject of
section~\ref{sec:fit} and, on these very events, of
figure~\ref{fig:fig6first}: refit, it reads $2.8\%$ at $T=1000$
against the classical $10.0\%$. One qualifier matters enough to
state here rather than in a caption: the wall is set by the
\emph{acceptance}, not by multiplicity alone. This whole section runs
at the wide fixed $\varepsilon=2$\,mrad of the benchmark
configuration, whose window admits several times more false
candidates than the formula acceptance of eq.~\eqref{eq:epsilon}; at
the formula acceptance the same $T=1000$ multiplicity is solved
outright ($99.98\%$ efficiency at a false rate below $0.1\%$,
section~\ref{sec:atscale}) — a direct measurement of
section~\ref{sec:toy:ham}'s claim that $\varepsilon$ is the most
consequential experimental knob.

The comb's amplitude picture explains the numbers. Under the comb
response the false population collapses to $\lesssim10^{-2}$ of the
true median (the passes reject everything off the lines), while the
true band stays tight around its classical profile: the $1/\lambda$
weighting inside the passes preserves the chain shape rather than
reshaping it as the single cosine does. Where the 1BQF splits the
true population and the classical solver drags a false tail across
the threshold, the comb simply leaves a gap.

\subsection{Hit inefficiency: the first appearance of fragments}
\label{sec:comb:drop}

Dropping $1\%$ of hits breaks $\approx5\%$ of tracks into fragments:
chains of one, two, or three segments whose spectra
(section~\ref{sec:toy:spectrum}) are \emph{not} on the four $P_4$
lines. The comb, by design, does not pass them. Per-class efficiency
under the production comb is $100\%$ on intact tracks and $0\%$ on
every fragment class, which is why its overall efficiency reads
$94.0\%$ rather than $97$--$98\%$ at $T=400$ under drop. Extending
the comb with passes at the fragment lines does not help: it
re-admits their false spectral twins wholesale (a $P_2$ fragment and
a false pair are the same matrix, figure~\ref{fig:twins}), so
fragments are exactly
degenerate with false pairs \emph{on this Hamiltonian}, the first
appearance of the twin degeneracy that
section~\ref{sec:fit} meets at full strength, and that
section~\ref{sec:fit:occ} shows an operator modification can
lift. Recovery on the unmodified Hamiltonian must come from
information a spectral filter
cannot see, namely hit-level constraint information, and that is
classical post-processing, outside the scope of this work.\footnote{An earlier version of this study paired a
hit-occupancy recovery gate with the comb alone; it was retired as
mission creep (no other solver received the same help, and the tool
is orthogonal to the spectral question). Classical post-selection is
developed and reported separately in the programme's occupancy
study.}

\subsection{Resource scaling and the depth ladder}
\label{sec:comb:resources}

Measured on the shared events: total width 21 qubits at $T=50$ to 29
at $T=1000$; success probability $P_{\rm anc}\approx0.44/T$; walk
calls per amplified repetition
$d\cdot\pi/(4\sqrt{P_{\rm anc}})\propto d\sqrt T$. Two findings
sharpen the budget. A half-resolved comb at $d=16$ matches the
$d=40$ metrics up to $T=700$ at an approximately $T$-independent
$P_{\rm anc}\approx6\%$, about fifty total walk calls, flat in $T$,
because partially-merged passes still cover the four lines while
rejecting the bulk. And the degree axis is not monotonic:
intermediate degrees ($d\approx20$--$32$) can cliff at the
efficiency-first working point through Chebyshev fit pathologies, so
every production degree must be validated, not interpolated.

\subsection{Spectral twins: a degeneracy no response can lift}
\label{sec:comb:limits}

The most important structural limit on the whole filter family
deserves its own name and its own picture. Call two segment clusters
\emph{spectral twins} when their compatibility graphs are isomorphic:
the same number of segments, connected in the same pattern, regardless
of where the segments physically are or whether they belong to a real
track. Because $A$ acts block-diagonally over connected components,
each cluster contributes a sub-block of $A$, and twin clusters
contribute \emph{the same sub-block up to relabelling} (permutation
similarity). The consequence is the \emph{twin degeneracy}: for every
function $f$, $f(A)\mathbf{b}$ assigns twin clusters identical
amplitudes, because permutation similarity commutes with matrix
functions and $\mathbf{b}$ is uniform on every cluster. No response,
fitted or otherwise, and no classical amplitude scoring, can tell
twins apart: whatever passes one passes the other.

Figure~\ref{fig:twins} draws the pair of twins that will matter most
in this work. A true two-segment fragment (three surviving hits of a
broken track) and a false pair (two fake segments that happen to
continue one another inside the acceptance) are physically completely
different objects; but both are two segments sharing one hit, both
produce the same $2\times2$ sub-block of $A$, and both therefore sit
on exactly the eigenvalue lines $s\mp1$ with exactly the same
amplitudes. On clean events twins are rare (an exhaustive scan of the
$T\le200$ events finds none; $0.6\%$ of true segments by $T=400$ with
the acceptance widened to $4$\,mrad), but the degeneracy they carry is
what ultimately caps the design, and under noise they return, as a
population, in section~\ref{sec:fit}.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\captionsetup{width=0.92\textwidth}
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{spectral_twins.pdf}
\caption{Spectral twins. A true fragment of a broken track (top) and
an accidental false pair (bottom) have isomorphic compatibility
graphs, hence identical $2\times2$ sub-blocks of $A$, hence exactly
the same eigenvalues and amplitudes. Every function of $A$ treats
them identically; this is the twin degeneracy, and under noise these
twins become a population large enough to cap segment efficiency
(section~\ref{sec:fit}).}
\label{fig:twins}
\end{figure}

Two further structural results complete the clean-toy picture. The
\emph{shared-line theorem}: the odd modes of a contaminated
five-segment chain coincide exactly with the $s\pm\sqrt3$ lines of
the three-pronged hub $K_{1,3}$, so no comb can null both populations
independently; pass menus trade one against the other with a fixed
exchange rate. And the \emph{mirror tax}: a chain-reflection symmetry
forces the false extension of a recovered cluster to the
recovered-true amplitude level, so recovery passes always buy their
efficiency at a false-rate premium. Together these results closed the
clean-toy design question. The production comb is optimal up to
provable exceptions, and further progress must come from changing
the operator or leaving the spectral domain, which is exactly where
the rest of this work goes.

\section{The heavy-noise events}
\label{sec:noise}

From here on the operating point is the heavy configuration:
$\sigma_{\rm scatt}=10^{-4}$, $\sigma_{\rm res}=20\,\mu$m, hit drop
$1\%$, at $T=200$, with the acceptance set by the kink formula to
$\varepsilon=6.3$\,mrad. Three reps of every event are used
throughout; where a single event is dissected, it is rep~0.

The acceptance width is the story. At $\varepsilon=2$\,mrad a false
segment rarely finds an angular partner; at $6.3$\,mrad the
compatibility graph fills with small false structures. Two facts
about that graph, both established by direct decomposition of the
solved events, control everything downstream:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item \textbf{There is no giant tangle.} The compatibility graph at
heavy $T=200$ splits into $869$ nontrivial connected
components of at most $17$ segments, floating in a sea of
$155{,}371$ isolated segments (rep~0). Noise, at this operating
point, buys many small motifs rather than one big mess, and small
components mean the entire spectrum is computable exactly, component
by component, with dense eigensolvers. Every spectral statement in
sections~\ref{sec:occupancy}--\ref{sec:fit} is exact, not
approximate. Figure~\ref{fig:tangles} shows what these components
actually look like in the detector: the component-size distribution,
and real examples drawn from the event, from a pure-false pair to
the largest seventeen-segment tangle. Seen at the micron scale of
their internal kinks, the ``tangles'' are bundles of near-collinear
competitors, exactly the structures the widened acceptance
admits.
\item \textbf{The false actives are motif-borne.} At the classical
efficiency-first working point the event has $2238$ active segments
of which $1454$ are
false; those false actives live in $707$ components that contain,
between them, only $142$ true segments. Ninety percent of the
false-active population sits in essentially pure-false motifs
(pairs, triples, short bridges), not entangled with true tracks at
all. Spectrally, this population lands exactly where the motif
dictionary of figure~\ref{fig:motifs} says it must:
figure~\ref{fig:pileup} shows the measured per-mode solution weight
of the event against $\lambda$, with the false weight piled up on
the pair line $s-1$ and the triple lines $s\pm\sqrt2$, interleaving
but not touching the four true lines.
\end{enumerate}

The solver scoreboard at this point makes uncomfortable reading. At
the efficiency-first working point the classical false rate is
$0.65$; the 1BQF's is $0.65$ as
well, since the notch's one zero still annihilates the isolated bulk
while every false \emph{active} is by definition coupled, off the
line, and untouched. The clean-event comb, designed for a $2$\,mrad
world where false structures barely exist, fails too: the
wide acceptance populates false motif lines (figure~\ref{fig:pileup})
inside its pass windows. The important statement here is the
diagnosis: at heavy noise all three solvers fail for the same
reason, a false population that has moved onto spectral lines the
designs were never told about, and the cure, developed in
section~\ref{sec:fit}, is to measure those lines and refit the
response to them. The classical literature also offers two
Hamiltonian terms as candidate cures, occupancy and bifurcation. The
next two sections take them in turn; neither behaves the way its
classical reputation suggests, and neither can be judged separately
from the refit.

\section{The occupancy term}
\label{sec:occupancy}

\subsection{The term}

Denby's original network~\cite{denby1988} constrains each hit to be
used by at most one selected segment per role. The quadratic form of
that constraint, per-hit \emph{and per-role} occupancy (a hit's
departing and its arriving segments are counted separately), adds
$E_{\rm occ}=\alpha\sum_h\sum_{r\in\{\rm in,\,out\}}(o_h^{\,r}-1)^2$
with $o_h^{\,r}$ the summed activation of the segments using hit $h$
in role $r$, which at the matrix level reads
\begin{equation}
A_{\rm occ}=A+4\alpha I+2\alpha B_{\rm all},\qquad
\mathbf{b}\to\mathbf{b}+4\alpha\mathbf{1},
\label{eq:occ}
\end{equation}
where $B_{\rm all}$ couples every pair of segments sharing a hit in
the same role: two segments departing the same hit, or two arriving
at it. Consecutive segments of one track share their interior hit in
opposite roles and are not coupled. On an isolated chain the attractor moves to
$(\delta+4\alpha)/(\gamma+\delta+4\alpha)$; on an event, as shown
below, it does not.

That role structure has an exact consequence worth deriving before
any experiment: \emph{on every pure chain the occupancy term is
nothing but the substitution} $(s,\delta)\to(s{+}4\alpha,\,
\delta{+}4\alpha)$. No same-role pair lives inside a chain, so
restricted to a chain of $m$ segments eq.~\eqref{eq:occ} reads
$(s+4\alpha)I-P_m$ with bias $\delta+4\alpha$, and the entire
closed-form vocabulary of section~\ref{sec:toy:levels} survives
verbatim. The ladder of eq.~\eqref{eq:ladder} generalises with
$\bar x'=(\delta+4\alpha)/(s+4\alpha-2)$ and
$r'+1/r'=s+4\alpha$:
\begin{equation}
x_j=\bar x'\left[1-\frac{r'^{\,j}+r'^{\,m+1-j}}{1+r'^{\,m+1}}\right],
\label{eq:laddocc}
\end{equation}
so at $\alpha=0.05$ the attractor sits at
$1.2/4.2=0.2857$, the $P_4$ outer and inner levels rise from
$4/11,\,5/11$ to $0.4052,\,0.5017$, and every chain eigenvalue line
of figure~\ref{fig:motifs} shifts rigidly by $+4\alpha$. On an
isolated chain, that is. An event is not a collection of isolated
chains: every hit carries $T-1$ false candidate segments per role, so
the same-role sums the term penalises are, to within $0.2\%$,
independent of which hit one looks at, and the term's effect on the
solution collapses to a mean field. Measured at $T=200$ and
$\alpha=0.05$, every segment amplitude, true or false, is the base
amplitude multiplied by one number, $0.1087$ (its 5th--95th
percentile range across segments is $0.1087$--$0.1088$ on clean
events and $0.108$--$0.110$ under noise), and the isolated-false level
sits at $(\delta+4\alpha)/(s+4\alpha+4\alpha(T-1))=0.027$, not at
the chain-only $0.286$. The atlas does not move as a block; it
shrinks as a block. What the term changes is the \emph{ranking}
inside the shrunken atlas, through the small per-hit departures from
the mean field, and that is what the efficiency-first working point
reads: the false rate at $99\%$ efficiency is unchanged on clean
events ($0.29\%$ both ways) and falls from $44\%$ to $35\%$ at
moderate and from $65\%$ to $30\%$ at heavy noise. Every occupancy
result below is read at that working point and stands; only the
closed-form ladder, exact for an isolated chain, must not be read as
a prediction of event amplitudes.

What it does touch is exactly the structures the base Hamiltonian
cannot see. A same-role pair with no continuation edge, invisible to
$A$ (both segments sit on the isolated line), acquires the
$2\alpha$ coupling and solves to $(\delta+4\alpha)/(s+6\alpha)
=0.2791$ at $\alpha=0.05$ — \emph{below} the shifted attractor by
$\approx 2\alpha\delta/s^2$, with its degenerate line split to
$s+2\alpha$ and $s+6\alpha$. This is the analytic seed of the
degeneracy lifting measured in section~\ref{sec:atscale}: the
invariant that twins must match grows from the compatibility graph alone
to the pair $(C, B_{\rm all})$, so two structures are twins only if
they agree in their chain shape \emph{and} in their hits' same-role
degrees — and the hit drop that creates a true fragment has just
lowered those degrees. Isomorphism becomes rare instead of generic.
The same algebra also prices the term's cost at scale: the
same-role $k$-clique at a single hit contributes $k{-}1$ lines at
$s+2\alpha$ and one at $s+4\alpha+2\alpha(k{-}1)$, so the top of the
spectrum grows linearly with the busiest hit's degree. That linear
growth is the span wall that section~\ref{sec:atscale} measures
($\lambda_{\max}\approx143$ at $T{=}700$, $202$ at $T{=}1000$), and
it is why in-matrix occupancy will emerge there as a low-multiplicity
technique.

\subsection{Classically it works; the notch it destroys}

On the heavy $T=200$ benchmark event the in-matrix occupancy term at
$\alpha=0.3$ does exactly what Denby promised: the classical
efficiency-first false rate falls from $0.650$ to $0.210$ at
efficiency $0.991$. If this work were classical, the section would
end here with a recommendation.

For the 1BQF it is a disaster, and the mechanism is worth spelling
out because it generalises. The notch's power is that isolated false
segments sit exactly on its zero. The occupancy coupling
$2\alpha B_{\rm all}$ connects precisely those segments to their
co-hit neighbours, which is its job, and thereby moves them off the
zero. On the benchmark event the co-hit false bulk, notched to
amplitudes $\sim10^{-19}$ under the base Hamiltonian ($99\%$ of it
below $10^{-17}$), re-emerges under $A_{\rm occ}$ at amplitudes on
the true band: its 99th percentile rises to $1.5\times10^{-3}$
against a true median of $1.2\times10^{-3}$. The rank separation
collapses (1BQF AUC $0.996\to0.634$) and no threshold rescues it;
the full sweep shows every occupancy-1BQF operating point dominated
by the base-1BQF curve. Nor does the self-coupling $\gamma$ offer an
escape: the notch tracks the diagonal, distance-to-notch is
$\gamma$-invariant, and at the window-restoring $\gamma=236$ the
occupancy system is strictly worse. Post-selection tells the same
story: the occupancy term diverts the ancilla-conditioned
probability mass almost entirely onto false segments.

\centering
\captionsetup{width=0.90\textwidth}
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{dp_occupancy_proof.png}
\caption{The occupancy proof (heavy $T=200$): (a) the same co-hit

\label{fig:occproof}

The mechanism generalises to any \emph{fixed} response: a spectral
filter earns its living from populations pinned to known eigenvalues,
and any Hamiltonian term that couples a pinned population unpins it.
For the 1BQF the verdict really is final, because the notch has no
further freedom to spend: its response family is exhausted by the
single parameter $t$, the retune $t=\pi/s'$ is already included
above, and figure~\ref{fig:occproof}(c) shows the $\gamma$ direction
offers no escape either. As a Hamiltonian term, occupancy is poison
to a notch, and the classical intuition for the term does not
transfer to it.\footnote{The occupancy constraint can also be applied
entirely outside the Hamiltonian, as a classical hit-uniqueness
post-selection applied evenly to every solver's output, deliberately kept out of this
work, whose subject is the spectral side.} But ``final for the
notch'' is not ``final for the filter family''. The fork term in the
next section will teach the general lesson of this work, that an
operator modification can only be judged together with a response
refitted to the spectrum it creates, and
section~\ref{sec:fit:occ} returns to the occupancy term with exactly
that machinery. The verdict there reverses: what destroys the
degree-one filter turns out to be the single most helpful operator
modification in the study once the polynomial is allowed to follow
it.

\section{The fork term and the moving spectrum}
\label{sec:fork}

\subsection{The term and its validity envelope}

The second Denby--Peterson penalty targets \emph{bifurcations}: two
segments claiming the same hit in the same role at small mutual
angle, the fork topology that produces the closely-competing
candidates a wide acceptance admits. The $\varepsilon$-windowed form
adds
\begin{equation}
A'=A+\beta\,B_{\rm fork}(\varepsilon_B),
\label{eq:fork}
\end{equation}
where $B_{\rm fork}$ couples co-hit same-role pairs within angular
window $\varepsilon_B$ (default: the acceptance itself), and the bias
$\mathbf{b}$ is untouched. Unlike the
occupancy term, the fork is surgical where it matters. On clean
events it touches $\approx2\%$ of segments, and provably no
pure-true chain carries a fork edge — so not just the true comb
lines but the \emph{entire true-chain solution} is exactly
$\beta$-invariant: the ladder of eq.~\eqref{eq:ladder} survives
untouched, atlas levels included. What moves is precisely the
topology the acceptance cannot resolve. An isolated competing pair
(two same-role segments inside the window, no continuation edge)
solves to $\delta/(s+\beta)=0.222$ at $\beta=0.5$, below the
$0.250$ attractor, with its degenerate line split to $s\pm\beta$:
the fork prices exactly the closely-competing candidates it was
built for. One caution comes from the same algebra: when a
\emph{false} prong forks onto a \emph{true} chain's outer segment,
the exact five-segment solve shows both ends move (the prong drops
far below the attractor, the outer chain level dips with it), so the
term's net effect on the true band is not sign-definite a priori —
which is one more reason the fork can only be judged together with
the response, the subject of section~\ref{sec:fork:nogo}. Finally,
$B_{\rm fork}\subset B_{\rm all}$, so under occupancy \emph{and}
fork a windowed pair carries the combined coupling $2\alpha+\beta$
and solves to $(\delta+4\alpha)/(s+6\alpha+\beta)=0.250$ at the
production knobs $(\alpha,\beta)=(0.05,0.5)$ — numerically back at
the base attractor, a coincidence of the operating point rather
than anything deep. Earlier classical-side work in this programme
established that the fork migrates contaminated-true spectral weight
back toward the comb bands; whether real solvers can collect that
migration is settled in section~\ref{sec:fit}.

Adding a positive semi-definite coupling widens the spectrum, and
the solver imposes two validity conditions with closed forms.
Positive definiteness requires $\gamma>\gamma_{\rm pd}=\gamma_{\rm
ref}-\lambda_{\min}$; the one-period condition of any cosine-family
filter (the top of the spectrum must not alias through the response
period) requires $\gamma>\gamma_{\rm win}=\lambda_{\max}-\gamma_{\rm
ref}-2\delta$, both extremes measured at a reference
$\gamma_{\rm ref}$ and shifted rigidly, since $\gamma$ enters only
the diagonal. At heavy $T=200$ the measured requirement is
$\gamma^*\approx3.4$ at $\beta=0.5$ and $\approx4.4$ at $\beta=1$.
The notch tracks the diagonal automatically ($t=\pi/s'$,
$s'=\gamma+\delta$), and the comb is rebuilt on the measured shifted
spectrum. Every fork result below carries these retunes; a
window-violating control ($\beta=0.5$ at $\gamma=3$) is kept in the
study to show they matter.

\subsection{The operator and the response are one design}
\label{sec:fork:nogo}

One methodological point, learnt the hard way, belongs here rather
than in a results table. Retuning a \emph{fixed} response to the fork
system, tracking the diagonal, rebuilding the comb on the shifted
domain, is not enough, because the retunes move the response's
centre and domain but never ask where the false weight now lives.
Evaluated that way, both Denby--Peterson terms read as regressions,
and the reading is a statement about the mismatch between an old
response and a new spectrum, not about the terms. The correct
procedure, used for every operator result in this work, is to
measure the modified system's false pile-up and refit the polynomial
to it: change the Hamiltonian, and the activations and their spectral
support move; when they move, the polynomial must be refitted to the
moved spectrum. Carrying out that refit, for the fork and then for
the occupancy term, is the subject of section~\ref{sec:fit}. (A
second, smaller point: at heavy noise the efficiency-first convention
degenerates for an unrefitted comb, whose contaminated-true
amplitudes collapse toward zero, so sections~\ref{sec:fit} onward
compare solvers at matched efficiency instead.)

\subsection{A Trotter aside that will matter on hardware}
\label{sec:fork:trotter}

Everything in this work so far treats the evolution $e^{iAt}$ as
exact. On hardware it is not: a quantum computer realises $e^{iAt}$
by a circuit, and the standard cheap construction, the
\emph{product formula} (or Trotterization), builds it from the
pairwise pieces of the Hamiltonian. The idea is simple: $A$ is a sum
of many small commuting-or-not terms, one per coupled segment pair,
and while $e^{i(X+Y)t}$ is hard, the product
$e^{iXt}e^{iYt}$ is easy, one small gate per term. If $X$ and $Y$
commute the product is exact; if not, it errs at order $t^2[X,Y]$,
and one hopes the accumulated error stays below the physics. The
1BQF circuit of ref.~\cite{trackhhl} is exactly such a per-pair
product.

The programme's measurements show where that hope holds and where it
breaks, and the split is clean (figure~\ref{fig:erf}). With
\emph{uniform} coupling weights (every term the same strength, as in
the step-kernel Hamiltonian of this work) circuit and exact
evolution agree to cosine similarity $\approx0.985$: the product
formula is safe. With \emph{heterogeneous} weights (the erf-weighted
angular kernel, or a fractional fork $\beta=0.5$ riding on unit
compatibility edges) the per-pair product scatters the readout badly
enough to dominate the physics: on the fork system the circuit reads
false rate $0.96$ where the exact evolution reads $0.73$, and on the
erf kernel removing the product formula alone collapses the false
rate from $0.83$ to $0.22$ at moderate noise. The rule of thumb for
hardware is that heterogeneous coupling weights break the naive
product formula, and circuit realisations of weighted Hamiltonians
need the structured synthesis route (edge-coloured pair ordering)
developed in the programme's direct-structural-synthesis study. All
fork and fit results in this work use exact evolution precisely so
that filter physics and circuit error are never conflated;
section~\ref{sec:limits:trotter} returns to what this means for
deployment.

\section{Fitting the polynomial to noisy events: roots at the
measured pile-up}
\label{sec:fit}

\subsection{Measuring the false pile-up}

The fit begins with a measurement no fixed design ever asked for:
where, exactly, does the false amplitude come from? Because
the heavy-noise compatibility graph has no giant component
(section~\ref{sec:noise}), the per-component eigendecomposition
gives, for every eigenvalue in the event, its exact content: how
much of the mode's weight lies on true segments, how much on false,
and how much of each segment's amplitude it builds. Under any
response $p$,
\begin{equation}
x_s(p)=\sum_i p(\lambda_i)\,\big(\mathbf{v}_i^{\!\top}\mathbf{b}\big)\,
v_i(s),
\label{eq:linresponse}
\end{equation}
which is nothing more than the spectral decomposition of the
filtered solution $p(A)\mathbf{b}=\sum_i
p(\lambda_i)\,\mathbf{v}_i\mathbf{v}_i^{\!\top}\mathbf{b}$ read off
at segment $s$. The two geometric factors are fixed by the event
alone, before any response is chosen: $\mathbf{v}_i^{\!\top}
\mathbf{b}=\delta\sum_s v_i(s)$ is the projection of mode $i$ onto
the uniform source (recall $\mathbf{b}=\delta\mathbf{1}$), and
$v_i(s)$ is that mode's amplitude on segment $s$. The response
enters only through the scalars $p(\lambda_i)$. Three consequences
follow, and the section rests on all three. First, each mode
deposits a definite \emph{formation weight}
$(\mathbf{v}_i^{\!\top}\mathbf{b})\,v_i(s)$ onto every segment, so
each segment's amplitude is a sum of contributions labelled by
eigenvalue: a histogram of that weight against $\lambda$ is the
spectral pile-up of figure~\ref{fig:pileup}. Second, each mode
carries a definite true/false \emph{content}, the fraction
$\sum_{s\,\in\,\rm true} v_i(s)^2$ of its weight landing on true
segments, so the false part of the amplitude can be attributed to
specific eigenvalues rather than to the solver. Third, and
decisively for what follows, $x_s$ is \emph{linear} in the values
$\{p(\lambda_i)\}$: freeze the geometry, treat the response samples
as the unknowns, and every segment amplitude is a fixed linear
combination of them. The question of where the false amplitude
comes from is therefore well posed and exactly answerable. The
answer, on the heavy
$T=200$ events, is concentrated to a degree I did not expect. The
false-active formation weight piles up on the \emph{pair line}
$\lambda=s-1$: on the three events measured, that single bin carries
$1122$--$1378$ weight units against $4$--$10$ units of true weight,
with the triple lines $s\pm\sqrt2$ next; the overall bin-level
overlap between false-active and true formation is $0.014$--$0.023$
in cosine. The false population is not smeared. It is parked on the
eigenvalue lines of identifiable two- and three-segment motifs,
almost none of which are shared with true formation.

\subsection{Fitting the response}

Because eq.~\eqref{eq:linresponse} is linear in the response,
choosing the response is a least-squares problem. Represent $p$ by
its values on a grid of narrow bins in $\lambda$ (width
$\Delta\lambda=0.02$, a few hundred bins spanning the spectrum);
these binned values are the free parameters, and by
eq.~\eqref{eq:linresponse} each segment amplitude is a fixed linear
combination of them, $x_s(p)=(D\mathbf{p})_s$ with $D$ the design
matrix that collects the formation weights into bins. The response
is then the solution of
\begin{equation}
\min_{p}\ \underbrace{\sum_{s\,\in\,\rm false}x_s(p)^2}_{\text{purity}}
\;+\;\mu\underbrace{\sum_{s\,\in\,\rm true}
\big(x_s(p)-x_s^{\rm cls}\big)^2}_{\text{efficiency}} ,
\qquad |p|\le1 .
\label{eq:fit}
\end{equation}
The first term drives the false-segment amplitudes toward zero; the
second pins the true-segment amplitudes to the values
$x_s^{\rm cls}$ the classical inversion gives them, so the fit
cannot manufacture a clean false spectrum simply by discarding true
segments. Both terms are quadratic in the binned values, so the
minimiser solves the linear normal equations
\begin{equation}
\big(D_F^{\!\top}D_F+\mu\,D_T^{\!\top}D_T\big)\,\mathbf{p}
=\mu\,D_T^{\!\top}\mathbf{x}^{\rm cls},
\label{eq:normal}
\end{equation}
with $D_F,D_T$ the rows of $D$ for false and true segments, and the
$\mathcal{O}(10^5)$-strong isolated-false line at $\lambda=s$
carried into $D_F$ as one heavily weighted row so it is held at the
notch. The bound $|p|\le1$ is the QSVT realizability constraint of
section~\ref{sec:qsvt}: only a response bounded by one on the
spectrum can be implemented as a walk. The single knob is $\mu$,
which trades the two terms against each other: $\mu\to0$ maximises
purity at the cost of the true band, $\mu\to\infty$ recovers the
classical efficiency at the cost of the false spectrum, and sweeping
it traces the efficiency--purity frontier the results below report.
No root position is chosen by hand: zeros appear where false weight
concentrates because that is what the objective rewards. The fitted
response reproduces the comb's passes at the (contamination-shifted)
true lines and drives deep roots through $s-1$ and $s\pm\sqrt2$:
precisely the polynomial that no clean-event design could have
anticipated, because the root locations are properties of the
measured noise.

Three conclusions follow.

First, the expected ordering is restored. Below efficiency
$\approx0.985$ the fitted quantum filter sits under the classical
baseline everywhere: $0.096$ against $0.124$ at $\eff=0.98$, $0.061$
against $0.124$ at $\eff=0.97$. The claim this section set out to
test, that the false rate should still be lower for the quantum
filters once they are fitted, holds.

Second, the fork earns its keep, but only through the refit. On the
fork system the fitted response
reaches $0.046$ at $\eff=0.97$, beating the fitted no-fork system's
$0.061$, while the classical solver on the same fork system reads
$0.292$. The operator knob does exactly what the spectral-migration
analysis promised, provided the response follows it: changing the
Hamiltonian without refitting the polynomial is not a half-measure,
it is worse than doing neither
(section~\ref{sec:fork:nogo}). The standard-notch 1BQF also benefits
from the fork
below the degeneracy limit ($0.145$ against $0.199$ at $\eff=0.98$): the fork
pushes coupled-false modes away from the passband even when the
only zero sits at $s'$.

Third, the wall at $\eff\ge0.99$ is real on these systems, and now
has a name. At the strict $99\%$ point everything, classical
included, floods back to $\far\approx0.65$. The per-component
decomposition locates the cause exactly: about $2\%$ of \emph{true}
segments, a couple of dozen per event, counted directly and
consistently on all three events, are spectral twins of the false
motifs, in exactly the sense of
section~\ref{sec:comb:limits} and figure~\ref{fig:twins}. They are
the hit-drop fragments of section~\ref{sec:comb:drop}: a broken
track's two-segment piece \emph{is} a pair, sitting on the pair
line, in a pure-true component, plus a handful of contaminated mixed
chains. Any response with a root at $s-1$ kills them alongside the
false pairs; their population exceeds the $1\%$ efficiency budget;
and once the working point must dip below their suppressed
amplitudes, the entire suppressed false band comes back above
threshold. This is the \emph{efficiency ceiling set by twin
fragments}: the noisy-event form of the twin degeneracy,
no longer a curiosity but the binding constraint. It is a
ceiling because it caps how far the efficiency can be pushed, and it
is set by twins because the fragments and the false pairs it confuses
are, to any function of $A$, the same object. The robust statement is the measured wall
itself: on the base and fork systems every solver holds
$\eff=0.98$ and none holds $\eff=0.99$, on all three events. On
\emph{those} Hamiltonians no function of $\lambda$ can cross it,
but the degeneracy belongs to the operator and not to the
event, and the next subsection shows an operator that lifts it.
\label{sec:fit:degeneracy}

\subsection{The occupancy term, refitted}
\label{sec:fit:occ}

Section~\ref{sec:occupancy} ended with an issue: the occupancy was measured on the fixed 1BQF targeting the central, isolated, eigenvalue, a filter with no freedom to follow the
spectrum the term creates, and the fork has since taught us that
operator terms can only be judged with a refitted response. The
occupancy system makes the refit technically harder, the co-hit
coupling $2\alpha B_{\rm all}$ merges the compatibility graph into a
giant component, so the exact per-component measurement behind
eq.~\eqref{eq:fit} is unavailable, but not impossible: a degree-$d$
response needs no eigendecomposition, because the moment vectors
$T_k(X)\mathbf{b}$ are computable by sparse matrix--vector recursion
and the amplitudes are linear in the Chebyshev coefficients. The fit
of eq.~\eqref{eq:fit} then becomes an exact least-squares problem in
$d{+}1$ coefficients, over \emph{every} segment of the event
including the isolated bulk. Run identically on the base and
occupancy systems (same algorithms, same events, only the Hamiltonian
differs), it settles the question.

The result of section~\ref{sec:occupancy} contradicts
(figure~\ref{fig:occfit}). At matched efficiency $0.98$ the directly
fitted response reads $0.085$ on the base system and $0.050$ on the
occupancy system at $\alpha=0.05$; at $0.97$, $0.071$ against
$0.039$. The term that destroys the notch roughly halves the false
rate of the fitted polynomial, while the classical solver on the
same systems sits at $0.12$ throughout. Two structural findings come
with the headline. First, \emph{small coupling wins}:
$\alpha=0.05$ beats the classically-motivated $\alpha=0.3$
($0.050$ vs $0.067$ at matched efficiency $0.98$), because the
occupancy term stretches the spectral span ($6.7\to43\to242$ for
$\alpha=0\to0.05\to0.3$) and a fixed degree budget loses resolution
proportionally; the spectral benefit saturates long before the
classical amplitude benefit does. Second, and most strikingly,
\emph{the occupancy coupling breaks that ceiling}. One of the
three events is held at the ceiling on the base system, its fitted
response cannot do better than $0.654$ at matched efficiency
$0.985$, because its fragment twins are exactly degenerate with
false pairs, and on the occupancy system the same event fits to
$0.062$. The mechanism is the twin degeneracy read in reverse: the
twin argument requires isomorphic components, and the co-hit
coupling embeds every motif in its hit-sharing neighbourhood, which
is different for a fragment of a real track than for an accidental
false pair. The embedding breaks the isomorphism, the degeneracy
lifts, and a response fitted to the new spectrum separates what was
provably inseparable on the base Hamiltonian. The ceiling of
section~\ref{sec:fit:degeneracy} is therefore a property of the
\emph{base} Hamiltonian, not of the problem.

One engineering note: the degree budget
that collects the occupancy win is larger than the base system's
(the span costs resolution; most of the gain arrives by degree
$80$--$160$ at $\alpha=0.05$). Like every fitted result in this
section, the fit itself is mechanical, the roots land on the
enumerable motif structure the operator produces, so the reversal
is a property of the Hamiltonian, not of a tuning; folding it into
the single fixed response of
section~\ref{sec:limits:generalization} is part of that
enumeration.

\subsection{Why the 1BQF specifically cannot follow}
\label{sec:fit:rootbudget}

A degree-one filter owns one zero, and the measurement says the
heavy false spectrum has two mandatory root locations: the isolated
line at $s$ ($\mathcal{O}(10^5)$ segments) and the pair line at
$s-1$ (the active pile-up). The study's control makes the conflict
vivid. Retuning the notch onto the pair line ($t=\pi/(s{-}1)$) does
root the pile-up, and releases the isolated bulk, which re-enters
at amplitude $|\cos(st/2)|\,\delta=0.5$, on top of the true band,
for a false rate of $0.995$. The 1BQF's zero is spoken for.
Covering both lines is degree two; covering the triple lines as
well is degree four and up; and that, stated as a root budget, is
in my opinion the cleanest justification for the whole QSVT
programme that the toy has produced.

\section{The operator axis at scale: degree, density, and the loss
budget}
\label{sec:atscale}

The preceding sections established the method — measure the modified
spectrum, refit the response — one operator at a time. This section
reports the systematic campaign that crosses the two axes: every
operator (base $A$, occupancy $\alpha=0.05$, fork $\beta=0.5$, and
the combination) against every polynomial degree ($d=4$ to $160$,
response refitted at each), at all three noise configurations
(clean, moderate, heavy; formula $\varepsilon$ throughout), on the
same stored events with the same matched-efficiency working points,
ten event repetitions per cell. The classical solver and a matched
1BQF — the cosine $|\cos(\pi\lambda/2s')|$ realised as a degree-60
Chebyshev on the same operator — are computed on the same events, so
every comparison in this section is like for like.\footnote{All
numbers in this section derive from
\texttt{QSVT/Efficiency\_Frontier/} outputs; the degree scan is
\texttt{06\_degree\_scan.csv}, the density scan
\texttt{03\_highT\_frontier.csv} + \texttt{06b}, the loss budget
\texttt{05\_loss\_budget.csv}. Classical post-processing (the
hit-uniqueness gate studied elsewhere in this programme) is out of
scope: every number is a property of the operator and the function
applied to it.}

improves. The marginal-gain plot (figure~\ref{fig:degmarg}) is the
operational summary: past the onset, one more degree step is worth
nothing, and the budget question \emph{which degree} reduces to
\emph{which operator}.

Two reference points sharpen this. First, the matched 1BQF: on base
$A$ it reads $1.000$ (clean), $0.488$ (moderate), $0.004$ (heavy)
at the $1\%$ budget — and exactly $0.000$ on \emph{every} occupancy
operator at \emph{every} noise level, clean included. The
degree-one filter does not merely degrade under the occupancy
spectrum, as section~\ref{sec:occupancy} showed; it dies entirely,
while the refitted $d{=}4$ polynomial on the same operator keeps
clean events perfect. Second, the \emph{unfitted} production comb
run over the same grid: on the occupancy operators it reads
$0.487$ (moderate) and $0.000$--$0.004$ (heavy) where the fitted
response reads $0.990$ and $0.935$. A fixed response on a moved
spectrum is not a weaker version of the method; it is a different,
failing experiment — the strongest quantitative form of
section~\ref{sec:fork:nogo}'s doctrine that the operator and the
response are one design.

\subsection{The loss budget: where the missing efficiency lives}
\label{sec:atscale:budget}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\captionsetup{width=0.95\textwidth}
\includegraphics[width=0.98\textwidth]{xiv_loss_budget.pdf}
\caption{(a) The efficiency lost at the $1\%$ false-rate budget,

Every true segment missed at an operating point is assigned to one
of three named channels: \emph{twin loss} — the segment's
filtered amplitude agrees with a false segment's under \emph{every}
member of a set of independent probe responses, which by the twin
degeneracy of section~\ref{sec:comb:limits} certifies graph
isomorphism; \emph{ripple loss} — recovered by another degree of
the same family; \emph{threshold-overlap loss} — the remainder.
The channels sum to the total loss by construction, and
figure~\ref{fig:budget} shows the result. On base $A$ at moderate
noise the best fitted response reaches $0.974$ at the $1\%$ budget
with zero ripple loss and zero threshold-overlap loss: \emph{the deficit is one hundred
percent twins}, and no function of $\lambda$ on the unmodified
operator can do better — the honest-negative bound of
section~\ref{sec:fit} promoted from a scan result to a proof. The
operator modifications act on exactly this channel. The twin
fraction of true segments falls from $3.6\%$ (base) to $0.76\%$
under occupancy at moderate noise, and — the decisive number — from
$87\%$ to $1.3\%$ at heavy noise, where on base $A$ the twin flood
forces a false rate of $0.633$ at the $99\%$-efficiency point for
\emph{every} solver, classical included. The no-go knobs earn their
verdicts in the same currency: shrinking the acceptance
($\varepsilon$-scale $1.5$) \emph{creates} twins ($11\%$ of trues)
by stripping couplings, and moving $\gamma$ moves the ceiling the
wrong way. Section~\ref{sec:occupancy}'s closed forms say why the
occupancy term is the twin-breaker: the invariant that twins must
match grew from the compatibility graph to the pair
$(C,B_{\rm all})$.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

I collect here, deliberately and in one place, everything that
stands between the results above and a deployable algorithm. Each
limitation comes with its measured size and, where we have one, its
escape route.

\subsection{The occupancy win does not survive density}
\label{sec:limits:occdensity}

The strongest configuration of section~\ref{sec:atscale} — the
occupancy operator with its refitted response — is, as measured, a
low-multiplicity technique. Its spectral span grows linearly with
the busiest hit's degree (the $k$-clique line of
section~\ref{sec:occupancy}), so a fixed-degree response loses
resolution $\propto\mathrm{span}/d$ and the moderate-noise
advantage decays from $0.9905$ at $T{=}200$ to nothing by
$T{=}700$. What the $T{=}200$ result establishes is the \emph{value}
of breaking the twin invariant, not a recipe that scales. The
untested escape routes are a multiplicity-dependent coupling
($\alpha\propto1/\deg_{\max}$, pinning the span), a
degree-normalised form of the co-hit term, and any operator that
distinguishes fragments from false motifs without coupling entire
co-hit cliques. Separately, the erf-kernel operator leaves the one
piece of pure response headroom in the study: its twin loss is the
smallest measured ($0.25\%$ at moderate noise) but its loss is
threshold overlap ($1.3\%$), the one channel a better-shaped
response can still buy back.

\subsection{Realizability of the fitted response}
\label{sec:limits:realizability}

An earlier version of this study believed realizability was the
bottleneck, and the false alarm is worth recording because the
resolution strengthens the result. The binned optimum of
eq.~\eqref{eq:fit} has features of width $\approx0.02$ on a spectral
span of $\approx7$; a Chebyshev series \emph{reproducing that
specific function} needs degree $d\approx2W/h\approx700$, and truncated
series fitted to it at $d\le240$ performed terribly. But reproducing
the binned optimum is the wrong problem. Fitting the objective of
eq.~\eqref{eq:fit} \emph{directly in the degree-$d$ coefficient
space}, amplitudes are linear in the Chebyshev coefficients, so
this is the same least-squares problem in $d{+}1$ unknowns, finds
realisable responses that match the unlimited-degree optimum already
at the production degree
(figure~\ref{fig:fit}d: $0.099$ at $d=40$ against the binned
optimum's $0.096$ at matched efficiency $0.98$ on the base system).
The measured discrimination of section~\ref{sec:fit} is therefore
available at circuit-affordable degree; nothing about the polynomial
family was ever in the way, only the indirect two-step fit.

A structural escape remains available on top: the same
no-giant-component fact that made the spectrum measurable makes the
problem block-diagonal. Solving
per connected component (a classical $\mathcal{O}(n)$ preprocessing
step) replaces one $4T^2$-dimensional filter problem by hundreds of
$\le17$-dimensional ones, on which a degree-$\le17$ interpolating
polynomial reproduces any response exactly, and as a bonus the
register shrinks to $\lceil\log_2 17\rceil+2$ qubits and the
success probability per cluster becomes $\mathcal{O}(1)$. The cost
is honesty about what remains quantum: per-cluster solving helps
the classical solver identically, and the quantum claim reduces to
the resource profile of section~\ref{sec:qsvt:resources} on
whatever cluster sizes survive preprocessing.

\subsection{One response for all events}
\label{sec:limits:generalization}

On this toy the fit is not a statistical learning problem, and no
train-and-test machinery is needed to understand it. The structures
an event can contain are enumerable, chains, hubs, bridges and
their contaminated combinations, each pinned to closed-form
eigenvalue lines, and the fit is mechanical: the roots land on
the motif lines because that is where the false weight must sit.
The root positions are therefore universal ($s-1$ and $s\pm\sqrt2$
are motif properties, not event properties). What does vary between
events is the contaminated-true pass positions, which chase each
noise realisation's particular contaminated modes: a response taken
verbatim from one event and applied to another reads $0.63$--$0.99$
false rate against $0.04$--$0.10$ on its own event, entirely
because its narrow passes sit at the wrong contaminated positions.
A production design is then an enumeration exercise, not a fitting
one: roots pinned to the motif lines, passes wide enough to cover
the contaminated-true spread of the ensemble. Building that single
fixed response, and measuring what its wider passes cost against
the per-event ceiling of section~\ref{sec:fit}, is the programme's
clear next step. On noisier data, real geometry, variable track
length, denser contamination, the structure will no longer be
enumerable by hand, and how to fix the response there will need
closer thought (section~\ref{sec:limits:geometry}).

\subsection{Trotterization on hardware}
\label{sec:limits:trotter}

Everything above uses exact evolution. On hardware, the 1BQF's
product-formula circuit is exact only in the uniform-coupling case;
the moment coupling weights differ (erf-weighted kernels,
fractional fork strengths) the naive per-pair formula scatters the
readout by amounts that dominate the physics
(section~\ref{sec:fork}). The structured-synthesis route
(edge-coloured pair ordering, developed in the programme's DSS
study, which also reaches 1BQF width for the comb via generalized
QSP over $e^{-iAt}$) restores exactness at matched cost and is, on
present evidence, mandatory for any weighted Hamiltonian on
hardware.

\subsection{Hardware resources: HHL, the 1BQF, and the QSVT side by
side}
\label{sec:limits:resources}

The three algorithms of this lineage are best compared in a common
currency, because on this problem they genuinely share one: all
three are built from repeated calls to the same controlled
time-evolution $e^{\pm iAt}$, synthesised gate-by-gate from the
Hamiltonian's pair structure (the direct-structural-synthesis
construction of ref.~\cite{trackhhl}: one Givens unit per coupled
segment pair, a CNOT ladder plus one multi-controlled rotation,
transpiled to a \{CX, $R_z$, $\sqrt X$, $X$\} basis). What
distinguishes the algorithms is only \emph{how many} calls they
make: the 1BQF interferometer makes two; the degree-$d$ comb,
realised as a Fourier comb over the same primitive, makes $d$; and
HHL wraps the evolution in phase estimation, making
$\approx2(2^{n_c}{-}1)$ calls for an $n_c$-bit clock register, an exponential price paid for amplitude information the selection
task never uses, which was the founding observation of the 1BQF.
Table~\ref{tab:resources} collects the measured numbers. Widths are
exact register counts; two-qubit gate counts are measured on the
stored events (the multi-controlled rotations transpiled, the CNOT
ladders counted from the measured Hamming distances, $k\approx
1.5$--$2.3\sqrt{N}$ coupled pairs); the accompanying one-qubit
$R_z/\sqrt X$ counts are of the same order as the CX counts (each
ladder CX is dressed by $\mathcal{O}(1)$ single-qubit rotations)
and are subdominant in every error budget, so CX is quoted. The

What error rates make each column viable? Run uncorrected, a
circuit of $N_2$ two-qubit gates survives with probability
$\approx(1-p_2)^{N_2}$, so the usable budget is $N_2\lesssim1/p_2$.
At today's best two-qubit fidelities ($p_2\approx10^{-3}$) that
budget is $\sim10^{3}$ gates: the per-cluster 1BQF
($\approx2\times10^{3}$ CX on the largest cluster, far less on
typical ones) sits exactly at this edge, which is why it is the
member of the family that has run on real
devices~\cite{trackhhl}. The per-cluster comb needs
$p_2\approx10^{-4}$--$10^{-5}$, one to two orders beyond present
hardware; every global column is out of uncorrected reach
entirely.

Beyond that budget the requirement becomes fault tolerance, and the
arithmetic is standard~\cite{fowler2012}. A distance-$d_c$ surface
code patch turns physical error $p$ into logical error per
operation $p_L\approx0.1\,(p/p_{\rm th})^{(d_c+1)/2}$ with
threshold $p_{\rm th}\approx10^{-2}$, at a cost of
$\approx2d_c^{2}$ physical qubits per logical qubit. Demanding
$p_L\lesssim1/N_2$ at $p=10^{-3}$: the per-cluster comb
($N_2\approx10^{4}$, 6 logical qubits) needs $d_c\approx7$,
i.e.\ $\sim10^{3}$ physical qubits, a near-term fault-tolerant
demonstrator. The global comb ($N_2\approx2.5\times10^{7}$, 24
logical qubits) needs $d_c\approx13$, $\sim10^{4}$ physical
qubits; global HHL, another order deeper, needs
$d_c\approx15$ on a wider register. Because the code distance
grows only logarithmically in depth, all the global routes land
within a factor of a few of $10^{4}$ physical qubits; the real separations between the columns are wall-clock time (the call
counts) and the per-cluster row. Two honest caveats: these are
logical-resource estimates from measured circuit counts and
standard surface-code scaling, not a compilation study; and the
Givens rotations carry arbitrary angles, so in a fault-tolerant
run the dominant cost would shift from the CX counts quoted here
to magic-state distillation for the rotation synthesis. The
practical reading of the table is the same from either end: the
per-cluster route of
section~\ref{sec:limits:realizability} is where this family meets
hardware first, the 1BQF already inside the uncorrected budget and
the comb one hardware generation behind it.

\subsection{A block encoding priced by hits, not matrix entries}
\label{sec:limits:blockencoding}

One assumption sits underneath every gate count in
table~\ref{tab:resources}, and relaxing it is the most promising
resource lever the programme has found. That assumption is how the
Hamiltonian enters the circuit: the \emph{block encoding}, the
unitary whose top-left corner is $A/\alpha$ and on which every
walk call is built. The per-call cost in the table is priced the
expensive way. The
$6.2\times10^{5}$ CX evolution call carries one circuit element per matrix nonzero, and so does every literature block encoding the
programme has benchmarked on these matrices (FABLE and its sparse
variants, explicit sparse-oracle circuits~\cite{camps2023},
dictionary encodings): all of them pay $\Omega(T^{2})$ gates per
call, a circuit as large as the classical matrix. But $A$ is a
derived object. The event itself is $5T$ hits,
$\mathcal{O}(T\log T)$ bits of information, and the matrix is a
fixed geometric function of them: two segments couple exactly when
they share a hit and their kink angle passes the acceptance of
eq.~\eqref{eq:epsilon}. A block encoding that queries \emph{hits}
rather than matrix entries (a quantum read-only memory over the
$5T$ sorted hit coordinates, fixed-point extrapolation arithmetic,
and a comparator against $\varepsilon$, assembled in the standard
state-preparation-pair form) costs $\mathcal{O}(T)$ gates per
call instead of $\mathcal{O}(T^{2})$. The programme's
block-encoding study has validated this construction on the stored
events: the hit-window enumeration reproduces every accepted
coupling exactly (coverage $1.0$ at every $T$ from $5$ to $1000$,
clean and noisy events alike), the subnormalization sits at the
local-occupancy floor ($\alpha=2$--$4$ up to $T=1000$, the same
class as the best entry-priced encodings, near the provable
$\lVert C\rVert$ lower bound), and the modelled per-call cost at
$T=400$ is $1.6\times10^{4}$ CX against $2.4\times10^{6}$ for the entry-priced route, two orders of magnitude, and growing with
$T$. Combined with the metric-validated comb degree (12--20
suffices on the base Hamiltonians; the production $d=40$ is
conservative), an entire hit-oracle comb solve costs
$3.3\times10^{5}$ CX at $T=400$: less than a single entry-priced
evolution call in table~\ref{tab:resources}. The price is width:
the memory workspace adds $\approx47$--$49$ qubits over the
21-qubit native floor, so the offer on the table is qubits for depth: roughly $3$--$4\times$ the register buys $4$--$7\times$
less depth per solve at equal or better false rate. Two further
measured facts complete the picture. The hit coordinates need $12$
bits of fixed-point precision, and this is a hard cliff rather
than a slope: at $10$ bits enough $\varepsilon$-boundary couplings
flip that the \emph{classical} solve on the same quantized matrix
breaks too, so the cliff is a property of the acceptance geometry,
not of the encoding. And the erf-weighted kernel costs nothing
extra, because the coupling weight is an arithmetic function of
the kink angle the oracle already computes, where the entry-priced dictionary encoding sees its subnormalization explode
on continuous values. None of this touches the twin degeneracy,
which is representation-independent; it moves only the hardware
accounting, and it moves it in the comb's favour.

\subsection{Success probability and readout}
\label{sec:limits:readout}

The filtered state must still be post-selected and read out, and it
is worth being explicit about what that costs, because it is the
largest single overhead in the whole scheme
(figure~\ref{fig:readout}). The LCU post-selection succeeds when the
index and dilation registers both return zero, which happens with
probability $P_{\rm anc}$ of eq.~\eqref{eq:psucc}; physically,
$P_{\rm anc}$ is the fraction of the input state's norm that
survives the filter. Because the uniform input spreads over all
$4T^2$ segments while only $\mathcal{O}(T)$ of them carry surviving
amplitude, $P_{\rm anc}$ falls as $1/T$: measured
$\approx0.44/T$ for the comb and estimated $\approx0.25/T$ for the
1BQF, i.e.\ thousandths at $T=1000$. Left alone this would mean
thousands of repetitions per useful sample. Amplitude amplification
(the standard Grover-type trick) reduces the repetition count from
$1/P_{\rm anc}$ to $1/\sqrt{P_{\rm anc}}$, restoring a $\sqrt T$
scaling of total walk calls per useful sample, and active-set
readout (sampling until the $\mathcal{O}(T)$ surviving segments are
each seen) gives the measured $d\cdot T^{1.5}\log T$ walk-call
budget against the classical $d\cdot T^{2}$ flop budget: a
square-root-class separation for that readout task, and no more
than that.

\subsection{Classical simulability of the response family}
\label{sec:limits:dequant}

Every response function in this work can be applied classically by
the Chebyshev recursion in $\mathcal{O}(d\cdot{\rm nnz}(A))$ flops,
seconds at $T=1000$. The discrimination physics (combs, roots,
degeneracies) is solver-independent mathematics of the segment
Hamiltonian, and this work's contribution to it stands whether or
not a quantum computer is ever involved. The quantum claim is
confined to the resource profile: width logarithmic in problem
size, walk calls $d\sqrt T$ against classical $dT^2$ for the
readout task above, and a hardware-native route to the same
amplitudes. Readers allergic to quantum advantage claims may read the whole work as classical spectral-filter engineering with a
quantum implementation appendix; nothing in the physics sections
would change.

\subsection{The toy's geometry, and what a real detector changes}
\label{sec:limits:geometry}

Finally, the largest caveat of all: the four-line comb exists
because this toy pins every true track to five hits. Real VELO
tracks have variable length, and a companion study on real Run-3
event geometry found the true spectrum smeared into a band, the
$P_4$ comb keeping only $40\%$ of true segments, and a
short fragments ($m\le2$ tracks indistinguishable from noise
at the segment level) capping all segment-level solvers, while the
fork transfer and the band-plus-notch redesign behaved as the toy
predicts. Figure~\ref{fig:run3} shows the measured amplitude
pile-up on the real geometry, side by side for the three solver
designs: where the toy's amplitude atlas (figure~\ref{fig:atlas})
has discrete levels, the real geometry smears the true population
into a continuum, the fragment population ($m\le2$, orange) sits
inside the false bulk for every solver, and the redesigned
band-plus-notch response reproduces the toy's qualitative
separation on the findable ($m\ge3$) population. The honest
statement of scope is that this work's method
is response engineering on whatever spectral structure the geometry
provides, demonstrated exhaustively on a geometry that provides a
lot of it; the measurement loop of section~\ref{sec:fit} (measure
the pile-up, fit the response, locate the degeneracy) is
geometry-agnostic even where the specific comb is not.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This work set out to finish a thought that the one-bit quantum
filter started. The 1BQF's insight was that track finding does not
need a linear-system solution, only a spectral separation, and that
one cosine with one zero buys the largest separation in the problem
at almost no quantum cost. Promoting that cosine to the full QSVT
polynomial family converts algorithm design into response design,
and response design turns out to be a measurement-driven loop:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item Read the spectrum the problem gives you. On clean events the
detector geometry hands you four exact eigenvalue lines, and a
forty-degree comb pinned to them beats the classical solver at the
wide $2$\,mrad benchmark acceptance up to $T\approx400$ tracks, every
solver read at the threshold that keeps $99\%$ of its true segments.
Beyond that the clean-event coefficients must be refit to the events,
and then the same polynomial converts the segment selection at
$T=1000$ from a $10.0\%$ false rate (classical) to $2.8\%$: a factor
of $3.6$ fewer fakes in the selected sample. At the formula
acceptance the same multiplicity is solved outright — $99.85\%$
efficiency at zero measured false rate (section~\ref{sec:atscale}).
\item When the problem changes, measure where the false weight went,
and put roots there. At the heavy-noise operating point
($\sigma_{\rm res}=20\,\mu$m, $1\%$ hit drop, $T=200$) the false
weight piles up on the pair and triple motif lines, and the response
refitted to that measurement reads a false rate of $0.061$ at
matched segment efficiency $0.97$ where the classical solver on the
same events reads $0.124$, half the fakes at identical
efficiency, on every event tested, at every efficiency below the
twin-fragment ceiling.
\item Judge every Hamiltonian modification together with the
response fitted to it, never separately. Both Denby--Peterson terms
destroy the degree-one filter, and both help the refitted
polynomial: the fork system fits to $0.046$ at matched efficiency
$0.97$ (against $0.061$ without the fork), and the occupancy system
at small coupling $\alpha=0.05$ fits to $0.039$, the best number
in the study, produced by the same term whose coupling collapses
the 1BQF's separation (AUC $0.996\to0.634$). Operator and response
are one design, not two.
\item Know what the spectrum cannot see, and know that the
operator can change what that is. Spectral twins bound every
function of $\lambda$ on a fixed Hamiltonian, classical scoring
included; at heavy noise the true fragments of broken tracks are
twins of the false pairs, and on the base and fork systems they
cap every solver at segment efficiency $\approx0.98$: pushing to
$0.99$ floods the selection back to a $\approx0.65$ false rate for
everything. But the degeneracy belongs to the operator and not to
the event: the occupancy coupling embeds each motif in its
hit-sharing neighbourhood, breaks the twin isomorphism, and the one
event that was degeneracy-limited at $0.654$ on the base system fits to
$0.062$ on the occupancy system at the same matched efficiency.
\end{enumerate}

The open problems are, I think, unusually crisp for this field: a
single fixed response for the whole event ensemble, on this toy
an enumeration over the finite motif structure (roots on the motif
lines, passes wide enough for the contaminated spread) rather than
a fitting problem, with the degeneracy-lifting occupancy system folded
in; per-cluster realization as the structural
alternative to global filtering; edge-coloured synthesis for
weighted couplings on hardware; and the transplant of the whole
loop onto real detector geometry, where the structure is no longer
enumerable by hand, the response question needs closer thought, and
the first measurements say the method survives even though the comb
does not. Every cloud has
a silver lining: the same noise that broke the clean-event comb
handed us a false population parked on nameable eigenvalue lines,
and a filter family with exactly enough roots to meet it.
